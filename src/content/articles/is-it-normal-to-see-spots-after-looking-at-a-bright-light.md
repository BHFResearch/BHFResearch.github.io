You glance at a window, a phone flash, or the glare off a car, and a spot follows your gaze. It may be a dark coin, a pale halo, or a color that does not belong — green where the light was white, or a magenta ghost after a green sign. Blink and it is still there. Look at a blank wall and it is clearer. A minute later it has faded, and the room looks ordinary again.

That floating patch is usually an afterimage. The retina has been briefly overloaded in one small region, and it needs a short recovery before that patch reports light the way the rest of the eye does. It is a normal cost of a visual system built to notice contrast, not to stare into a lamp. What is not ordinary is a spot that stays fixed in the same place in the world, a curtain sliding across the view, or lightning-like flashes that appear in the dark with no bright source at all.

## What a Light Spot Feels Like

People use “spots” for several different events. Sorting them is the useful part.

The common version moves with the eye. You looked at something bright, then a patch of the same shape hangs in the center of whatever you look at next. On a white page it often looks dark. On a dark surface it can look light for a second, then dark. If the source was colored, the ghost is often the opposite color. It fades over seconds, sometimes a minute or two after a camera flash or a hard glance at the sky near the sun. Both eyes may show it if both were exposed. One eye shows it if you caught the glare with one eye more than the other.

A second version is a brief sparkle or star at the moment of the flash, gone almost as soon as you notice it. That is closer to the pressure sparks some people see when they rub the lids, described in <a href="https://research.betterhealthfacts.com/articles/is-it-normal-to-see-stars-when-you-rub-your-eyes/">seeing stars when you rub your eyes</a>, except the trigger is light rather than pressure.

A third version is not an afterimage. Threads, dots, or cobwebs that drift when the eye stops, then lag behind when the eye moves, are usually floaters in the vitreous gel. They were there before the bright light. The glare only made them easier to notice against a bright background. That pattern is covered in <a href="https://research.betterhealthfacts.com/articles/is-it-normal-to-see-floaters-in-your-vision/">floaters in your vision</a>.

A fourth version never had a light source. Zigzag lines, shimmering heat-haze, or a blind patch that spreads over five to twenty minutes, then a headache, is more like migraine aura than a bleached retina.

## Why a Bright Light Leaves a Ghost

The retina does not photograph the world. It bleaches pigment to measure light, then rebuilds that pigment in the dark.

Cone cells in the center of the retina hold opsin pigments tuned to different wavelengths. George Wald’s work on visual pigments showed that light changes the shape of those molecules so they can start a nerve signal. William Rushton later measured, in the living eye, how quickly cone pigments bleach in strong light and how they recover afterward. A small patch of retina that just faced a lamp has used up more pigment than the surrounding retina. Until that pigment is restored, the patch cannot signal brightness the same way. Against a pale wall the tired patch looks dark. That dark ghost is a negative afterimage.

The first moment can go the other way. For a fraction of a second the cells are still discharging, so the spot looks bright — a positive afterimage. Hermann von Helmholtz described both phases in his work on physiological optics in the nineteenth century. Jan Evangelista Purkinje had already mapped how afterimages change with the background you look at next. The spot seems to move because it is painted on the retina, not on the wall. Wherever the eye points, the bleached patch points there too.

Color ghosts use a second trick. Ewald Hering argued that color is coded in opposing pairs — red against green, blue against yellow, light against dark. Stare at a green exit sign and the green channel in that patch fatigues. Look away and the unfatigued red-magenta side of the pair wins for a little while. The ghost is not a stain on the eye. It is a temporary imbalance in the comparison the retina is always making.

Kenneth Craik and later vision researchers showed that afterimages can also be shaped higher up, in circuits that adapt to contrast, not only in the pigment itself. That is why a sharp edge can leave a sharper ghost than a vague glare, and why the spot is easiest to see on a blank surface. A busy scene hides it. Closed lids can reveal a related wash of color and noise, which is a different everyday event, covered in <a href="https://research.betterhealthfacts.com/articles/is-it-normal-to-see-patterns-when-you-close-your-eyes/">patterns when you close your eyes</a>.

Recovery is usually quick because cones rebuild pigment faster than rods. A camera flash in a dim room can feel longer because the rods, which handle night vision, were also bleached, and rod recovery is slower. Horace Barlow’s work on retinal adaptation helps explain why the same flash is a nuisance indoors at night and almost nothing at noon. The system was already running in a bright setting.

## Common Triggers

Ordinary life supplies plenty of brief overloads.

A phone or camera flash at arm’s length. Oncoming headlights at dusk, especially if the road is otherwise dark. A welding arc or a halogen work lamp, even from the side. Sunlight bounced off snow, water, or a pale car. The sky near the sun when you look up to check the weather. A bright screen in a dark room, then a glance at a white wall. Stadium lights. A microscope or dental lamp if you are the one under it.

The spot is more obvious if you are tired, if the room afterward is plain and evenly lit, or if you keep checking for it. Checking does not create the bleach, but it keeps attention on a fading patch you would otherwise ignore.

## Less Common but Serious Patterns

Most light spots are local pigment fatigue. A few patterns are not, and they should not be watched at home for days.

A fixed blind spot that does not move when you look around, in one eye, can be a problem in the retina or the optic nerve rather than a temporary bleach. Optic neuritis, for example, can dim color and wash out the center of vision in one eye, often with pain when the eye moves. It is not a one-minute ghost after a lamp.

New flashes of light in the side vision, like a camera going off inside the eye, without any bright source, can be the vitreous tugging on the retina. A sudden shower of new floaters, a shadow like a curtain, or a missing wedge of the visual field needs same-day eye care. Those are the warning signs clinicians teach for retinal tear or detachment, not the signs of an afterimage.

A spreading zigzag that builds over minutes, with or without a headache afterward, fits migraine aura more than bleach. First-time aura after age fifty, aura that lasts more than an hour, or aura with weakness, trouble speaking, or double vision should be treated as urgent until a clinician says otherwise.

Pain, a very red eye, halos around lights with a brow ache, or sudden blur that does not clear after the spot fades are not afterimage behavior. Halos with headache and a hard eye can be acute angle-closure glaucoma. That is an emergency.

## Hidden Triggers

A few everyday factors make a normal ghost last longer or feel stranger.

Dark-adapted eyes. Walking out of a cinema into daylight, or checking a phone in a pitch-black room, hits a retina that has turned its sensitivity up. The same lamp is a bigger bleach.

One eye doing the work. If the other eye was closed or in shadow, the spot is obvious only when you cover the spared eye. People sometimes think the eye is damaged because the comparison is so stark.

Dry or watery eyes. Glare scatters more when the tear film is uneven, so the bleach can be a smear instead of a neat disc. The smear still moves with gaze and still fades.

Medications and eye drops that widen the pupil let more light in. A bright afternoon can feel harsher for a few hours after a dilated exam. That is expected, and sunglasses help.

Migraine-prone brains. Some people get a true afterimage and, separately, a low threshold for visual aura. The two can be confused if the only question asked is “did you see a spot?”

## When to Worry

Seek urgent eye care or emergency care if you notice any of these:

- A curtain, shadow, or missing chunk of vision
- A sudden burst of new floaters plus flashes
- Flashes in the dark with no light source, especially if new
- A spot that is still there hours later and does not move with the eye
- One eye suddenly dim, with pain when you move it
- Halos around lights with severe eye or brow pain, nausea, or a red eye
- After a blow to the eye, not just a bright light
- Weakness, slurred speech, or double vision with the visual change

A spot that matches the shape of the light, moves when you look around, and is gone within a few minutes is the ordinary pattern. You do not need a visit for every camera flash.

## Myths vs Facts

Myth: A spot after a bright light means the retina is burned.
Fact: True solar retinopathy comes from staring at the sun, usually for many seconds, not from a glance at a window or a phone flash. The ordinary ghost is pigment that will be rebuilt. Staring at the sun is still a bad idea. The afterimage is the warning, not the permission to keep looking.

Myth: If you can still see the spot with your eyes closed, it is in the brain and dangerous.
Fact: Closing the eyes just removes the background. The bleached patch is easier to notice. Helmholtz and later observers used closed lids on purpose to study afterimages.

Myth: Colored ghosts mean a migraine every time.
Fact: Opposite-color ghosts are Hering’s opponent channels doing what they do. Migraine aura tends to spread, shimmer, and last many minutes, and it does not require a lamp.

Myth: Rubbing the eye will clear the spot faster.
Fact: Rubbing adds pressure signals and can scratch the cornea. Time and ordinary light clear a bleach. Rubbing does not rebuild pigment.

## How to Manage It

Look away as soon as you notice glare. One hard glance is enough to start an afterimage. Lingering makes the bleach deeper.

Give the eyes a mid-bright surface, not darkness and not another lamp. A normal room lets cones recover without a second hit. Sunglasses outdoors, and a brimmed hat on snow or water, cut the dose before it arrives.

After a camera flash at night, expect a longer ghost. Avoid driving until the spot has faded and headlights look normal again. A minute of patience is cheaper than a missed turn.

If screens at night leave a smear, lower the brightness so the page is not a lamp in a cave. The pupils article on this site makes the same point from the other direction: indoor eyes are often wide open, so a small bright source lands harder.

Do not test the spot by looking back at the source. That is how a short ghost becomes a longer one.

## When to See a Doctor

See an optometrist or ophthalmologist promptly if afterimages start lasting much longer than they used to, if a blur remains after the spot fades, or if you are getting frequent unexplained flashes. Mention any new medications, recent eye dilation, migraine history, or a recent blow to the head or eye.

Same-day care is the right bar for curtain shadows, a shower of new floaters, or a fixed missing patch. Those are not waiting-room problems for next month.

## FAQs

### How long should a spot after a bright light last?

Most fade in seconds to a couple of minutes. A strong camera flash in a dark room can linger longer, sometimes several minutes, while rod vision recovers. A spot that is unchanged after an hour, or a blur that remains after the ghost fades, deserves an eye exam.

### Why is the spot a different color from the light?

The retina compares opposing colors. Fatigue one side of a pair and the other side briefly dominates. A green light often leaves a magenta or pink ghost. A bright white light more often leaves a dark or gray patch. Ewald Hering described this opponent pattern in the nineteenth century, and it has held up in modern cone physiology.

### Are afterimages the same as floaters?

No. An afterimage is fixed to your gaze and fades. Floaters drift, lag when you flick your eyes, and are still there tomorrow. Bright backgrounds make floaters easier to see, so people often notice both at once and assume the light created the threads.

### Can a phone flash damage your eyes?

A normal phone flash at arm’s length is an afterimage problem, not a burn. The dangerous pattern is staring at the sun or at a welding arc without protection. If a flash leaves a spot that clears, that is the expected bleach-and-rebuild cycle Rushton measured in cone pigments.

### Why do I see the spot more on a white wall?

A plain bright surface gives the tired patch nothing to hide in. The difference between bleached retina and fresh retina is the whole picture. A cluttered scene masks the same difference. That is also why clinicians sometimes ask you to look at a blank surface when they are checking a real blind spot.

### Should I worry if only one eye has the spot?

Not by itself. If one eye caught the glare, one eye gets the ghost. Cover the other eye and the spot is obvious. Worry more if the one-eye change includes pain with eye movement, lasting dimness, or a missing area that does not match any light you looked at.

## Conclusion

A spot that follows your gaze after a lamp, a flash, or a hard glance at the sky is the retina admitting it was briefly overloaded. Pigment bleaches, opponent channels tilt, and a ghost sits on the next surface until the chemistry catches up. Helmholtz, Hering, Wald, and Rushton were describing that recovery, not a disease. Let it fade, stop looking back at the source, and treat curtains, new flash-and-floater storms, or a spot that will not leave as a different problem entirely.

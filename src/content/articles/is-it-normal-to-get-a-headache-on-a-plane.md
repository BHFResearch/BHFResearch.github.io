You were fine at the gate. Somewhere after the seat-belt sign went off, a tight band settled behind one eye, or a dull ache started at the temples. By descent it is sharper, timed almost perfectly to the pressure change in your ears. A headache on a plane is common enough that headache specialists gave one version of it a name. Most of the time it is the cabin doing ordinary things to a head that expected sea-level air, a working nose, and a usual cup of coffee. A smaller share is a sinus that will not equalize, a migraine that travel woke up, or a pain that should be checked after you land.

## What This Symptom Feels Like

In-flight headache is not one sensation. People usually describe one of these patterns:

- A bilateral pressing or tight band that builds over an hour or two, worse if you skipped water or your usual caffeine
- A brief, severe, stabbing pain in one frontal or eye region that starts during ascent or, more often, landing, and fades within about half an hour of being on the ground
- A deep ache over the cheeks or forehead that tracks ear fullness and gets worse as the plane descends
- A familiar migraine: one-sided, light-sensitive, sometimes with nausea, triggered by sleep loss, skipped meals, or the glare of a window
- Neck-and-temple ache after hours of a dropped head and a seat that will not quite support the skull

The short landing pain is the pattern clinicians now call headache attributed to aeroplane travel. The slower band is usually cabin air, mild altitude, and habits. They can overlap on the same flight.

## Why the Cabin Gives You a Headache

A commercial cabin is not the outside air at cruising altitude. It is pressurized, but not to the pressure of the airport you left. Regulations and aircraft design generally keep cabin altitude at or below about 8,000 feet, roughly 2,400 meters, and many flights sit nearer 6,000 feet. That is a real drop in the partial pressure of oxygen. John B. West’s work on altitude physiology, and aviation physiologist John Ernsting’s accounts of cabin atmosphere, describe the same trade: enough oxygen for a healthy passenger to stay alert, not enough to feel exactly like sea level.

A Boeing cabin-altitude study led by J. Michael Muhm, published in the New England Journal of Medicine in 2007, found that oxygen saturation falls as simulated cabin altitude rises, and that some passengers report more discomfort at 7,000 to 8,000 feet than at lower cabin altitudes. For most healthy adults the drop is modest. For someone who is already short on sleep, anemic, fighting a cold, or sensitive to altitude, that modest hypoxia is enough to start a vascular headache. The International Classification of Headache Disorders already lists headache attributed to high altitude. A long-haul cabin is a mild, indoor version of the same physics.

Dry air stacks on top. Cabin relative humidity often sits near 10 to 20 percent. Work associated with Niren Nagda on aircraft cabin air quality documented how little moisture the ventilation system holds once bleed air has been conditioned. Dehydration does not have to be dramatic to worsen a pressure-type headache. It sits next to <a href="https://research.betterhealthfacts.com/articles/is-it-normal-for-your-eyes-to-feel-dry-on-a-plane/">gritty eyes in the same dry cabin</a> and a nose that has already stopped humidifying incoming air.

## The Landing Headache Specialists Named

In 2004 V. Atkinson and K. S. Lee described a severe headache strictly linked to aircraft descent. Later series from M. S. Berilgen and B. Müngen, and a larger clinical description from Federico Mainardi, Ferdinando Maggioni, Carlo Lisotto, and Giorgio Zanchin, outlined a repeatable picture: sudden, often one-sided pain in the forehead or around the eye, beginning during takeoff or landing, severe for minutes, and gone soon after the pressure change ends. The International Classification of Headache Disorders, 3rd edition, now includes headache attributed to aeroplane travel. It asks for at least two episodes tied to flight, severe pain, and resolution within about 30 minutes after the ascent or descent is over.

The leading idea is a sudden pressure mismatch in a sinus or nasal passage, not a brain emergency. A blocked ostium cannot vent as cabin pressure rises on descent. Local inflammation around trigeminal branches may explain the stabbing frontal pain. It is a cousin of the ear squeeze described by Bluestone and colleagues, which is why the same landing often brings both a blocked ear and a headache. If your ears will not clear, the article on <a href="https://research.betterhealthfacts.com/articles/is-it-normal-for-your-ears-to-feel-full-after-a-flight/">post-flight ear fullness</a> covers the tube side of the same descent.

This named headache is unpleasant and can be frightening the first time. In published series it is usually self-limited. It is not the same as a migraine that happens to start on a plane, and it is not a reason to assume a bleed or a clot because the pain was sharp.

## Common Triggers That Are Not the Altitude

Cabin pressure is only half the story. The rest is what travel does to a head that was already close to a headache.

**A cold, allergies, or a recent sinus infection.** Swollen lining narrows the sinus openings. Descent then becomes a squeeze. Pain sits over the cheek, brow, or between the eyes, and may linger longer than the classic airplane headache.

**Skipped caffeine.** If you drink coffee most mornings and the flight replaced it with a dry pretzel, withdrawal headache can start within 12 to 24 hours. It is a dull, bilateral ache with irritability, and it often eases after a normal dose.

**Alcohol and not enough water.** A drink in dry air speeds fluid loss. The headache the next sector is often the same one people get after a late night, not a new cabin disease.

**Neck posture and glare.** A seat that tips the skull forward loads the suboccipital muscles for hours. Window glare and a seatback screen add an eye-strain layer. That ache is postural. It eases when you stand in the jet bridge and look at the horizon.

**Sleep loss and an empty stomach.** Red-eye flights and missed meals are classic migraine primers. If your usual migraine trigger is skipped food, the flight did not invent a new mechanism. It simply removed the meal.

**A tight scarf, headphones, or a ponytail against the headrest.** External pressure on the scalp can trigger a compression headache in susceptible people. Loosening the band is the test.

## Less Common but Serious Causes

Most plane headaches are benign. A few patterns are not explained by cabin altitude or a sinus squeeze.

A sudden “worst ever” headache, especially with vomiting, confusion, weakness, trouble speaking, or a drooping face, needs emergency care after landing, or crew attention if it starts in flight and does not behave like your usual pain. A fever with a stiff neck is not a cabin headache. Visual loss, double vision, or a headache that is clearly different from any you have had before deserves a clinician, not another ibuprofen and a hope.

People with significant heart or lung disease, severe anemia, or sickle cell disease can tolerate cabin hypoxia less well. Headache plus chest pain, marked shortness of breath at rest, or confusion is a reason to tell the crew. A blood clot in the leg is a separate flight risk. It does not typically announce itself as a simple temple ache. Calf swelling, one-sided leg pain, and later chest pain or breathlessness are the clues, not the landing headache described by Mainardi’s group.

Carbon monoxide is sometimes blamed in popular writing. Modern transport-category aircraft ventilation makes that an unlikely explanation for an ordinary passenger headache.

## When to Worry

Get medical help promptly if a flight headache comes with any of these:

- Sudden maximal pain, fainting, new weakness, speech change, or confusion
- A seizure, or a headache after head injury on the way to the airport
- Fever, rash, or a stiff neck
- New neurologic symptoms that do not clear when the ears pop
- Pain that is still severe many hours after landing and is not your usual migraine pattern
- Headache plus chest pain or breathlessness out of proportion to a short walk up the jet bridge
- Repeated airplane headaches that are worsening, or pain on only one side with persistent nasal blockage and discharge after you are home

A brief frontal stab on descent that fades at the gate, or a dull ache that lifts after water, food, and a normal night, fits the common story.

## Myths vs Facts

**Myth:** Any headache in the air means you are dangerously short of oxygen.  
**Fact:** Cabin altitude is real, and Muhm’s group showed saturation falls, but healthy passengers usually stay in a range that causes discomfort rather than a medical emergency. The severe short landing pain is more about local pressure than about running out of air.

**Myth:** Airplane headache means you should never fly again.  
**Fact:** The ICHD picture is recurrent in some people and absent in most. A decongestant strategy, ear equalization, and hydration prevent or blunt many episodes. A clinician can help if it happens every flight.

**Myth:** Drinking extra water on the plane fully prevents it.  
**Fact:** Fluid helps the dry-air and caffeine-gap types. It does not open a blocked sinus ostium on descent.

**Myth:** A neck ache after a long flight is a blood clot.  
**Fact:** Clot symptoms are usually in the leg or, if a clot travels, in breathing and chest pain. Hours of a flexed neck produce a muscle headache.

**Myth:** Painkillers taken after the stab starts will stop an airplane headache.  
**Fact:** The attack is often over before a tablet is absorbed. People who get the landing pattern repeatedly sometimes do better with a clinician-advised dose before descent, not after the pain peaks.

## How to Manage It on the Next Flight

You do not need a new identity as a nervous flyer. You need a small pressure-and-fluid routine.

- Drink water from boarding onward. Match any alcohol with extra fluid, or skip the drink if you are already headache-prone.
- Keep your usual caffeine timing. A skipped morning coffee is a reliable self-experiment with a dull headache as the result.
- On descent, equalize early and often: swallow, yawn, or a gentle Valsalva. Do not wait for pain. Chewing gum helps some people.
- If you are congested, ask a clinician about a nasal decongestant or steroid spray before flying. Forcing a blocked nose through a cold is the setup for sinus squeeze.
- Aim the gasper vent at your chest or lap, not straight into your eyes and dry mucosa.
- Support the neck. A small roll in the lumbar curve and a head that stays over the shoulders beat three hours of chin-to-chest.
- Eat something ordinary. An empty stomach plus dry air is a poor combination if you also get <a href="https://research.betterhealthfacts.com/articles/is-it-normal-to-get-a-headache-when-youre-dehydrated/">a headache when you are dehydrated</a> or underfed.
- If the named landing headache is a repeat problem, talk with a clinician before the next trip. Published approaches include a pre-descent anti-inflammatory in selected patients. That is a personal medical decision, not a cabin hack.
- After landing, walk, look at a distant horizon, and give the pain half an hour before you decide it is still “the flight.”

## When to See a Doctor

See a clinician if airplane headache happens on most flights, if sinus pain and a blocked nose continue after you are home, if you have frequent migraine that travel reliably detonates, or if any red-flag feature showed up even once. Mention cabin timing precisely: ascent, cruise, or descent, one side or both, and how long it lasted. That history is what separates Mainardi and Zanchin’s landing headache from migraine, sinusitis, and the rare serious causes.

People with lung disease, heart failure, significant anemia, or sickle cell disease should ask before long flights whether supplemental oxygen or a different plan is appropriate. Headache in that setting is a clue about reserve, not just dry air.

## FAQs

**Is a headache during landing normal?**  
A brief, severe, one-sided frontal or eye pain that starts as cabin pressure rises and fades within about half an hour is a recognized pattern, headache attributed to aeroplane travel. It is miserable and usually benign. Pain with neurologic symptoms is not that pattern.

**Can cabin air really be dry enough to cause a headache?**  
Yes. Relative humidity in the teens, plus lower oxygen pressure at a cabin altitude of several thousand feet, is enough for a pressure-type headache in susceptible people. Muhm’s cabin-altitude work and Nagda’s air-quality measurements describe the setting. Individual sensitivity varies.

**Why does only one side hurt?**  
The landing form is often unilateral because one sinus ostium or one trigeminal territory takes the pressure hit. A migraine can also be one-sided. A brand-new one-sided headache with weakness or speech change needs urgent assessment.

**Will chewing gum stop it?**  
Gum helps some people equalize the ears and may reduce the pressure trigger. It will not fix dehydration, caffeine withdrawal, or a migraine that was already coming. Start equalizing before pain, not after.

**Should I worry about a clot if my head hurts after a flight?**  
A simple flight headache is not how a clot usually presents. One-sided calf swelling, leg pain, sudden breathlessness, or chest pain are the symptoms to act on. A headache alone, especially if it matches your usual pattern and settles, points back to cabin pressure, sinus squeeze, or travel habits.

**Can children get this?**  
Children can get ear and sinus squeeze on descent, and some get headache with it. The named airplane-headache series are mostly adults. A child with severe pain, persistent vomiting, or unusual drowsiness after a flight should be assessed rather than watched overnight without advice.

## Conclusion

A headache on a plane is usually the cabin’s mild altitude, very dry air, a sinus or ear that lagged on descent, or an ordinary trigger you brought on board: no coffee, no water, no sleep, a flexed neck. The short stabbing landing pain has a place in headache classification because it is real and repeatable, not because it means the flight damaged you. Equalize early, drink, keep your usual caffeine, and treat congestion before you fly. Pain that is sudden and maximal, paired with confusion, weakness, fever, or breathlessness, is the exception. That one does not wait for the next sector. It gets a clinician.

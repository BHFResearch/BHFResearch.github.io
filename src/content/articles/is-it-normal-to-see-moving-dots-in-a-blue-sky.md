You look up at a clear sky, not at the sun, and the blue is not empty. Tiny bright specks dart along short curved paths, speed up for a moment, then vanish. Look at a cloud, a tree, or the sidewalk and they disappear. That pattern has a name, and for most people it is a normal view of their own retinal blood flow, not a warning that the eye is failing.

## What These Sky Dots Look Like

The usual description is specific. The dots are small, bright, and quick. They follow squiggly paths rather than drifting like dust on the eye. Many people notice a faint darker tail behind each bright speck. They show up in both eyes, though you may only notice them when you cover one and look at an even blue field. A small patch in the very center of gaze often looks emptier, because the fovea has no capillaries of its own.

They are easiest to see against a uniform bright blue: open sky, a clear patch between clouds, or a blue screen held at a comfortable distance. They fade against a busy background. They do not stay in one spot when you look away, and they do not look like threads, cobwebs, or a gray curtain.

If the specks pulse a little faster after you climb stairs or hold your breath and release, that fits the same story. The motion is tied to the heartbeat, not to the weather and not to how clean the air is.

## Why a Blue Sky Shows Them

This is the blue field entoptic phenomenon, sometimes called Scheerer’s phenomenon. Richard Scheerer described the flying corpuscles against a blue field in 1924 and argued that people were seeing their own blood cells move. Later work pinned the bright speck on white blood cells, not on floaters in the vitreous gel.

Retinal capillaries are tiny. Red blood cells pack them almost wall to wall. Hemoglobin absorbs short blue wavelengths strongly, so a column of red cells looks relatively dark when the retina is flooded with blue light. A white blood cell is larger, paler, and does not absorb blue the same way. As it squeezes through, it leaves a short gap of clear plasma. That gap, and the leukocyte itself, reach the photoreceptors as a brief bright spot. The darker tail is the red-cell column catching up behind it.

Stephen Sinclair and colleagues tested that source question directly and supported leukocytes in the macular capillaries as the origin of the dots. Charles Riva and Benno Petrig then turned the same percept into a measurement. Their blue field simulation matched a person’s seen dots to a computer pattern and estimated how fast leukocytes travel in the macula. The speed rises and falls with the cardiac cycle. You are, in a quiet way, watching your pulse in the back of the eye.

The sky is a convenient lamp. It is bright, blue-rich, and even, and you can look at it without staring at the sun. Indoor white light is broader spectrum, so the contrast between red cells and the plasma gap is weaker. That is why the same capillaries are almost invisible at a desk and obvious on a clear day.

## How This Differs From Floaters

Floaters live in the vitreous, the gel in front of the retina. They are clumps of collagen or cells, and they drift when the eye moves, then lag and settle. They can look like threads, rings, or gray specks, and they show up on white walls, book pages, and screens, not only on blue sky. The article on [ordinary floaters](https://research.betterhealthfacts.com/articles/is-it-normal-to-see-floaters-in-your-vision/) covers that slower, gravity-tugged pattern.

Sky dots do the opposite. They move on their own, along capillary routes, even if you hold the eye still. They prefer blue. They do not leave a smear on a white page. A person can have both: a few classic floaters and, separately, the darting blue-field specks. The two do not mean the same thing.

Afterimages are different again. A bright window or phone can bleach photopigment and leave a spot that stays fixed in the visual field for seconds. Those spots are covered in the piece on [spots after a bright light](https://research.betterhealthfacts.com/articles/is-it-normal-to-see-spots-after-looking-at-a-bright-light/). Sparkles that appear only as you stand up, with a gray-out or a tunnel, are a blood-pressure dip to the retina, not leukocyte traffic. That pattern is described in [sparkles when you stand up too fast](https://research.betterhealthfacts.com/articles/is-it-normal-to-see-sparkles-when-you-stand-up-too-fast/).

## Common Reasons You Notice Them Now

Most eyes have always had this traffic. Attention is what changes.

- A clear day after weeks indoors. The first long look at open sky is when people “discover” the dots.
- Recovery from a screen day. The sky is the first even blue field you have stared at.
- A higher pulse. Exercise, caffeine, fever, or anxiety makes the specks dart faster, so they cross the threshold of notice.
- One eye closed. Covering an eye removes binocular rivalry and makes entoptic detail easier to see.
- A blue wall, a swimming pool, or a clear high-altitude sky. Same optics, different backdrop.
- New glasses or a dilated exam. A sharper or brighter retinal image can make an old phenomenon feel new.

None of those create the capillaries. They only make an old view easier to catch.

## When the Dots Are Not This Phenomenon

A few patterns need a different explanation.

A sudden shower of new floaters, flashes of light like a camera at the edge of vision, or a shadow coming in from the side can be a retinal tear or detachment. Those flashes are mechanical, from vitreous tugging on the retina, not from white cells in capillaries. They do not require a blue sky.

A fixed dark or missing patch, a zigzag fortification line that spreads over minutes, or vision that goes gray in one eye for several minutes is not Scheerer’s phenomenon. Spreading zigzags can be migraine aura. A curtain, a sector of vision gone, or painless one-eye gray-out needs same-day care.

Visual snow is a different constant. It looks like television static over every surface, indoors and out, not a handful of bright darts only on blue. New, persistent snow with trailing images or light sensitivity is worth an eye and neurology conversation, even though many cases are benign.

Dots only in one eye, only after a blow to the head, or paired with a severe headache, a new droop, or trouble speaking are not a sky trick. One-sided sudden visual change is a reason to be seen promptly.

## Myths vs Facts

Myth: The dots are dust, pollen, or something on the cornea. Fact: Surface debris moves when you blink and is visible against many backgrounds. Blue-field dots keep moving if you hold a blink and vanish on a patterned scene.

Myth: Seeing them means high blood pressure or diabetes. Fact: The phenomenon is normal in healthy eyes. Disease can change capillary flow, which is why researchers measure it, but noticing the dots on a hike is not a blood-pressure reading.

Myth: You should stare at the sun to “test” them. Fact: The sun is the wrong lamp. Blue sky beside the sun is enough. Looking at the sun can burn the retina and has nothing to do with this test.

Myth: If you can see blood cells, something has leaked. Fact: You are seeing the optical shadow and gap of cells that are still inside intact capillaries, not blood in the vitreous.

Myth: They will turn into floaters if you ignore them. Fact: They are a different compartment. Leukocyte traffic does not become vitreous collagen.

## How to Check That Yours Fit the Benign Pattern

You do not need a device. On a clear day, look at open sky well away from the sun, with a hand shading the brow if the glare is strong. Notice whether the specks are bright, quick, and curved, whether a small central zone is quieter, and whether they fade when you look at a tree or a page. Compare eyes one at a time. If both eyes match, the specks vanish off the blue field, and there is no flash or curtain, you have likely met your own macular capillaries.

A faster dart after a short walk is still the same phenomenon. Riva and Petrig’s work is one reason that speed-up is expected: leukocyte velocity in these capillaries tracks the pulse.

Skip the experiment if you have a new shower of floaters, a shadow, or one-eye vision loss. Those are not a sky-gazing question.

## When to See a Doctor

Book a routine eye exam if the dots are new to you and you have not had a dilated look in a while, especially after age 50, when vitreous changes are more common and worth separating from this benign percept. Go sooner, the same day if you can, for new flashes, a burst of floaters, a curtain or sector missing, pain with redness and halos, or a sudden change in one eye only. Add urgent care for vision loss with weakness, trouble speaking, or a thunderclap headache.

Bring a plain description: both eyes or one, only on blue or on every background, darting or drifting, any flash or shadow. That single contrast does most of the sorting.

## FAQs

### Is it normal to see moving dots only when the sky is blue?

Yes. Blue light is what creates the contrast. Hemoglobin in red cells absorbs those short wavelengths, so the pale leukocyte and its plasma gap look bright. Against white paper or a green tree the same cells are usually invisible.

### Do the dots mean I have floaters?

Not by themselves. Floaters drift and lag after an eye movement and show up on many backgrounds. Blue-field dots move on their own along short curved paths and prefer a uniform blue field. You can have both, and they are not the same finding.

### Why do they speed up when my heart is pounding?

Leukocytes in macular capillaries move faster as the pulse pressure rises. Charles Riva and Benno Petrig used that link to estimate capillary blood speed from the dots people reported. A quicker dart after stairs or caffeine is the expected direction, not a new disease.

### Should I worry if the center of my vision looks emptier?

A small quiet zone in the exact center fits the foveal avascular zone, the capillary-free pit used for sharp vision. A new missing patch off-center, a gray curtain, or a hole in reading vision is different and needs an exam.

### Can children see them too?

Yes. The capillaries are there early. Children often notice the specks on a bright day and then forget them. The concerning signs are the same at any age: new flashes, a shadow, or a sudden drop in vision.

### Will glasses or contacts make them worse?

They should not create them. A cleaner retinal image can make an old phenomenon easier to notice. Contacts can add their own smear if the lens is dry or dirty, but that smear moves with a blink and is not limited to blue sky.

## Conclusion

Moving dots on a clear blue sky are, for most people, a brief look at white blood cells passing through retinal capillaries. Scheerer named the view, Sinclair’s group tied it to leukocytes, and Riva and Petrig showed that the dart tracks the pulse. It prefers blue, it happens in both eyes, and it leaves when the background gets busy. Drifting threads on a white wall, flashes, or a curtain are a different conversation, and those deserve an eye exam rather than another look at the sky.

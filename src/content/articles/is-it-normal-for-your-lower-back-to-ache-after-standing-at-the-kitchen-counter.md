<p>You stand at the kitchen counter chopping vegetables or washing dishes and, after ten or fifteen minutes, a dull ache settles into the lower back. The feeling is extremely common and usually reflects the way a static upright posture loads the lumbar region when the hips and core are not shifting.</p>

<h2>What This Feels Like</h2>
<p>The ache is typically a low-grade, bilateral tightness or fatigue just above the belt line. It builds gradually rather than appearing as a sharp stab. Many people notice it eases as soon as they walk a few steps or sit briefly. It is distinct from the sharper pain that can follow a twist or lift.</p>

<h2>Why This Happens</h2>
<p>Standing still at a counter keeps the hip flexors in a relatively shortened position while the lower-back muscles work continuously to hold the trunk upright. Without the small postural sway and weight shifts of walking, the lumbar discs and facet joints experience a steady compressive load. Stuart McGill’s work on spinal mechanics has shown how prolonged static postures increase fatigue in the lumbar extensors even when the absolute load is modest.</p>

<h2>When to Worry</h2>
<p>Seek advice if the ache is sharp, radiates into the leg, is accompanied by numbness or weakness, follows a fall, or persists for more than a few days despite movement. Night pain that is not relieved by changing position also warrants evaluation.</p>

<h2>How to Manage</h2>
<p>Small changes reduce the load:</p>
<ul>
<li>Shift weight from one foot to the other every minute or two.</li>
<li>Place one foot on a low stool to reduce the lumbar curve slightly.</li>
<li>Take a 30-second walk around the kitchen every few minutes.</li>
<li>Keep the counter height comfortable so you are not leaning forward excessively.</li>
</ul>

<h2>FAQs</h2>
<h3>Why does it only happen at the kitchen counter and not while walking?</h3>
<p>Walking provides continuous small movements that unload and reload the lumbar tissues. Standing still does not.</p>
<h3>Does a soft mat under the feet help?</h3>
<p>It can reduce fatigue in the legs and feet, which indirectly lessens the compensatory tension in the back.</p>
<h3>Is this a sign of weak core muscles?</h3>
<p>Not necessarily. Even strong cores fatigue under prolonged static demand. Variety of posture matters more than strength alone.</p>

<h2>Conclusion</h2>
<p>An ache in the lower back after standing at the kitchen counter is a normal response to sustained static posture. Brief weight shifts and short walks usually keep the sensation from building.</p>

You clear your throat, or you cough once hard, and a bolt of pain arrives at the back of the head before the cough is even finished. It can feel like a snap, a burst, or a hot weight behind the eyes. Then, often within a minute, it is gone, and you are left wondering whether a cough is allowed to do that.

Sometimes it is. A hard cough is a full-body pressure event, and the skull feels that pressure. The pattern clinicians call primary cough headache is real, usually brief, and usually bilateral. It is also one of the everyday headaches that should not be shrugged off the first time it appears, because a similar burst can come from a tight spot at the base of the skull. The difference is not something you can settle by how dramatic the pain felt in the moment.

## What a Cough Headache Feels Like

The timing is the clue. The pain starts at the cough, the sneeze, the strain, or the laugh — not an hour later. It peaks almost at once. Many people describe it as explosive, electrical, or stabbing, often at the back of the head, sometimes across the forehead or at the crown. It may hit both sides, or it may favor one side.

Duration is usually seconds to a few minutes. The International Classification of Headache Disorders allows the pain to last from one second up to two hours, and some people are left with a dull ache after the sharp peak fades. It should not bring numbness, double vision, trouble speaking, or a spinning room that will not settle. Those extras change the story.

The same burst can follow other brief pressure moves: blowing the nose, straining on the toilet, lifting a heavy bag, bending to tie a shoe, or a hard laugh. That family of triggers is why older papers called it a Valsalva-manoeuvre headache. It is not the same as a headache that builds during a long run. Exertional headache and cough headache can look related on a bad day, but they are classified separately, and the exercise version has its own list of causes. A closer cousin is the brief sparkle some people see with a hard sneeze, covered in [is it normal to see stars when you sneeze or cough hard](https://research.betterhealthfacts.com/articles/is-it-normal-to-see-stars-when-you-sneeze-or-cough-hard/). Stars are a visual pressure trick. A cough headache is pain.

## Why a Cough Can Hurt the Head

A cough is a forced exhalation against a briefly closed glottis. Pressure in the chest and abdomen spikes, venous blood is pushed toward the head, and the pressure of the fluid around the brain rises for a second or two. The British neurosurgeon Bernard Williams measured those cerebrospinal-fluid pressure waves during cough and showed how quickly they travel up the spine. In an open system the wave passes and the pressure falls. If the channels at the foramen magnum — the opening where the brain stem meets the spinal cord — are crowded, the wave can get stuck, and the pain is sharper and more reliable.

Charles Symonds, writing in Brain in 1956, separated this “cough headache” from ordinary migraine and from headache brought on by longer exercise. Later series, including the work of Julio Pascual and colleagues, found the primary form mostly in people over 40, often with a male tilt in older clinic groups, though not in every series. The International Headache Society notes that primary cough headache is usually bilateral and posterior, and that it mainly affects people older than 40. A chest-clinic report found that about one in five patients who already had a troublesome cough also reported cough headache, so the trigger does not have to be rare for the pain to show up.

The pain is not the lung “sending” a headache. It is the brief mechanical load on pain-sensitive structures: dura, blood vessels, and the crowded tissues at the craniocervical junction. Frequency tracks the cough. A week of bronchitis can produce a week of bursts, then silence once the cough stops.

## The Version That Is Not Just a Cough

Here is the part that makes this symptom different from a charley horse or a clicky knee. In headache-clinic series, cough headache is secondary — caused by something structural — in a large minority of cases. The International Classification notes that the syndrome is symptomatic in about 40 percent of cases, and that most of those patients have a Chiari malformation type I, in which the cerebellar tonsils sit lower than usual through the foramen magnum. Other reported causes include posterior-fossa tumors, basilar impression, spontaneous intracranial hypotension, carotid or vertebrobasilar disease, and, rarely, aneurysm or reversible cerebral vasoconstriction.

That 40 percent figure comes from people who sought specialist care, not from everyone who winced once during a cold. Even so, neurologists treat a new cough headache as a reason to look, usually with an MRI of the brain and the craniocervical junction, rather than as a diagnosis made by history alone.

Clues that lean secondary, drawn from Pascual’s comparisons and from the Chiari headache criteria, include onset before about 40, pain that keeps lasting many minutes rather than a few seconds, dizziness or unsteadiness with the burst, occipital pain plus signs of brain-stem or spinal-cord trouble, and a story that has gone on for years instead of a few weeks or months. Primary cough headache, in the modern series, often runs an episode of weeks to a couple of years and then fades. Chiari-related pain can be the only clue for a long time. Tonsillar descent is also found in people who never have the headache, so a scan finding still has to be read in context.

Indomethacin, a strong anti-inflammatory, has a reputation for easing primary cough headache and failing when the cause is Chiari. That is a clinical hint, not a home test, and it is not a drug to start without a clinician.

## Common Triggers That Make an Ordinary Burst More Likely

A winter cold, post-nasal drip, asthma, reflux that tickles the larynx, or a habit of throat-clearing can supply the cough. Once the cough is frequent, each pressure spike is another chance for the ache. Constipation and heavy lifting do the same job without a cough at all. Dehydration and a skipped meal will not create this specific burst, but they can leave the head more complain-prone; the skipped-meal pattern is a different headache, described in [is it normal to get a headache when you don’t eat](https://research.betterhealthfacts.com/articles/is-it-normal-to-get-a-headache-when-you-dont-eat/).

High altitude, a recent flight, or a sinus full of pressure can make any head pain feel worse, though cabin headache and cough headache are not the same mechanism. Neck stiffness from sleep position can sit in the same neighborhood of the skull and confuse the picture the next morning.

## When to Worry

Seek urgent care if a cough headache arrives with a sudden “worst ever” quality that does not fade in minutes, fainting, a weak arm or leg, trouble speaking, new double vision, a seizure, or a stiff neck with fever. Those are not the ordinary one-second burst.

Book a prompt medical visit, and expect imaging to be discussed, if:

- The burst is new, even if it lasts only seconds.
- It keeps happening with cough, strain, or bending.
- It lasts longer than a few minutes, or a dull ache hangs on for hours every time.
- You are under 40 and this is a new pattern.
- You also notice unsteady walking, swallowing trouble, arm numbness, or dizziness tied to the cough.
- The pain is only on one side and getting more frequent.
- You have a known connective-tissue condition, a past aneurysm, or neurological symptoms you have been explaining away.

A single brief wince during a violent coughing fit, gone in seconds, with no other symptoms, is the everyday version. Repeating bursts are the version worth a scan, even when each one is short.

## Myths vs Facts

**Myth:** If it only lasts a few seconds, it cannot be serious.  
**Fact:** Both the primary form and Chiari-related cough headache are often very short. Duration alone does not clear you.

**Myth:** Cough headache means your blood pressure spiked into the danger zone.  
**Fact:** A cough does raise pressure briefly. The pain is more about intracranial pressure transmission than about a sustained blood-pressure disease.

**Myth:** It is just a sinus headache from the cold.  
**Fact:** Sinus pain tends to be facial, pressure-like, and tied to congestion. A cough headache is a snap at the moment of the cough, often at the back of the head.

**Myth:** Exercise headache and cough headache are the same thing.  
**Fact:** They share a Valsalva flavor, but primary exercise headache builds with sustained effort and has a different age pattern and treatment profile. More on that split is in [is it normal to get a headache after exercise](https://research.betterhealthfacts.com/articles/is-it-normal-to-get-a-headache-after-exercise/).

**Myth:** Everyone with a Chiari malformation has this pain.  
**Fact:** Many people with mild tonsillar descent never notice it. The headache is a reason to look, not proof of a diagnosis.

## How to Manage the Ordinary Pattern

Treat the cough, and many of the headaches leave with it. Hydration, humid air, and treating post-nasal drip or reflux reduce the number of pressure spikes. When you feel a cough coming, turning the head slightly or coughing in a more upright posture does not fix a structural problem, but some people find the burst less sharp if they are not bent double.

Avoid heavy straining while the pattern is active. A footstool during bowel movements, and not holding the breath to lift a box, removes two common non-cough triggers. Keep a short note of how long each burst lasts and what else you feel. That log is more useful to a clinician than a vague memory of “it hurt.”

Do not self-start indomethacin or daily painkillers to chase the bursts. Frequent painkiller use can add a rebound headache on top of the original one. If a clinician confirms a primary cough headache after imaging, they may discuss a short course of treatment. That decision belongs after the scan, not before it.

## When to See a Doctor

See a doctor for any new, repeating cough headache, even if you feel well between bursts. Ask specifically about an MRI that includes the craniocervical junction, not only a sinus view. Go sooner if the pain is lengthening, if you are younger than the typical primary age group, or if balance, speech, vision, or limb sensation have changed. Emergency care is for the thunderclap that will not pass, or for headache plus neurological deficits, fever, or collapse.

## FAQs

**Is a one-second head pain when I cough normal?**  
A single brief burst during a hard cough can be the mechanical pressure spike Williams described. If it repeats, it still deserves a medical look, because short pain is also the typical Chiari pattern.

**Why is it at the back of my head?**  
Primary cough headache is often posterior, where the pressure wave meets the foramen magnum and the upper neck. Occipital pain is also typical of Chiari-related headache, so location alone does not separate the two.

**Can laughing or straining on the toilet cause the same pain?**  
Yes. Any brief Valsalva maneuver can trigger it. The classification groups cough, straining, and similar maneuvers together.

**Will it go away if my cold goes away?**  
If the only change was a temporary cough and imaging is clear, the bursts often fade over weeks to months. A pattern that continues after the cough stops needs rechecking.

**Do I need a scan after one episode?**  
One isolated second of pain during a violent cough, with a full recovery and no other symptoms, is often watched. New or repeating episodes are the usual reason clinicians order an MRI. The International Headache Society emphasizes neuroimaging because a large share of specialist-clinic cases are secondary.

**Is this a migraine?**  
Migraine can worsen if you cough during an attack, but primary cough headache occurs only with the cough or strain, peaks immediately, and is not followed by a typical migraine hangover. A clinician can separate them by the trigger pattern.

## Conclusion

A cough is allowed to flick a brief pain through the head. The chest pressure spike is real, the fluid around the brain feels it, and in many older adults that burst is a primary cough headache that settles once the coughing does. The same snap is also the classic hint of a crowded opening at the base of the skull, which is why repeating bursts are not a wait-and-see symptom. Note the timing, skip the self-diagnosis, and let a scan sort the ordinary pressure wave from the one that has nowhere to go.

You step outside without gloves, and within a few minutes the backs of your hands look marbled. Pale rings sit next to dusky pink and faint blue patches, almost like a net drawn under the skin. Indoors, with a mug between your palms, the pattern fades. It can look alarming the first time you notice it in daylight. For most people it is a normal cold response of the skin’s blood vessels, not a clot and not the start of a circulation disease.

## What This Pattern Looks Like

Physiologic mottling, sometimes called physiologic cutis marmorata or physiologic livedo reticularis, is a lace-like or net-like color change. The lines are usually incomplete circles or a fishnet, a few millimeters to a centimeter across. Color runs from pink to violet to pale blue. It shows most on the backs of the hands, the forearms, the thighs, and sometimes the knees. Fair skin shows it more clearly because there is less melanin to hide the color of blood in the small veins.

It is usually painless. The hands may feel cool or slightly stiff, the way any cold hand feels. The pattern appears in cool air, air-conditioning, a cold car, or after washing in cool water, and it clears within minutes of real warming. Infants show a stronger version of the same net because their vessel control is still immature. Adults with pale skin often keep a milder version for life.

## Why Cold Skin Looks Net-Like

Hand skin is built to dump or save heat. Fingertips and palms are rich in arteriovenous anastomoses, short bridges that can send blood past the capillary bed when you need to cool, and shut when you need to keep heat in the core. Sympathetic nerves drive that shut-down with norepinephrine.

In cool air those nerves tighten the small arteries feeding the upper dermis. The horizontal venous plexus just under the surface stays relatively full. That plexus is arranged as a net, so the color you see follows the net: bluer where venous blood sits close to the surface, paler where the arteriole has clamped down. Dermatologists have described this net pattern since Salomon Ehrmann’s account of livedo reticularis in 1907. Thomas Lewis, mapping cold reactions in human skin, showed that the color change tracks local vessel tone, not damage to the skin.

Cold also makes the constriction stronger at the receptor level. Nicholas Flavahan and colleagues showed that cooling moves alpha-2C adrenoceptors to the surface of vascular smooth muscle, so the same nerve signal closes the vessel more tightly. That is useful for keeping core temperature up. It is also why a short walk to the car can marble the hands even when you do not feel deeply chilled.

If the cold is strong enough and lasts, many fingers later show a brief reopening, the hunting reaction Lewis described and Hein Daanen later measured as cold-induced vasodilation. The net may flicker, pink up in patches, then return. That cycling is a heat-saving compromise, not a sign that the vessels are failing.

Core temperature matters as much as the air on the skin. Lewis noted that cooling the trunk tightens hand vessels even if the hands themselves are covered. A thin jacket and bare hands is a reliable recipe for mottling. Anxiety can add the same sympathetic squeeze, which is why stressed hands look blotchy in a cool room even before you step outside. That overlap is the same system behind [cold hands when you are anxious](https://research.betterhealthfacts.com/articles/is-it-normal-for-your-hands-to-go-cold-when-youre-anxious/).

## How This Differs From Raynaud and From Illness

A marble pattern that fades with warmth is not the same thing as Raynaud phenomenon. Fredrick Wigley and others describe classic Raynaud as episodic, sharply bordered color change in the fingers: white, then often blue, then red on rewarming, usually with numbness or throbbing, set off by cold or emotion. The border is a line across a finger, not a lace over the back of the hand. A separate article covers [fingers that turn white in the cold](https://research.betterhealthfacts.com/articles/is-it-normal-for-your-fingers-to-turn-white-in-the-cold/).

Steven Dean’s reviews of livedo separate the harmless cold net from livedo racemosa, a broken, irregular, persistent pattern that does not care whether the room is warm. That broken pattern can accompany clotting disorders, autoimmune disease, or vessel inflammation, and it deserves a medical look. Ordinary cold mottling is complete or nearly complete rings, symmetric, and gone after warming.

A few lookalikes are worth naming so you do not mix them up:

- Chilblains are itchy or burning red-purple bumps after cold, damp exposure, not a flat net that vanishes indoors.
- Acrocyanosis is a more even, persistent duskiness of the hands or feet, often with clamminess, not a lace that comes and goes with the weather.
- Bier spots are small pale macules on a pink background, usually on the arms, and they blanch differently from a cold net.
- Sepsis mottling is a sick-person sign, with fever, confusion, or feeling severely unwell, not a pattern you notice while walking the dog.

## Hidden Triggers

Air-conditioning aimed at the desk, a steering wheel in a cold car, and wet hands in wind all pull heat off the skin faster than dry still air. Nicotine tightens skin vessels and can make the net darker. Decongestants and some stimulant medicines add sympathetic tone. Beta blockers can leave the hands cooler in some people. Amantadine is a known drug cause of livedo and is a reason to mention new mottling that does not fade.

Low body fat and low blood pressure make the venous color easier to see. So does fair skin. A long sit with hands in the lap can add a little dependent duskiness on top of the cold net. The same temperature-contrast system that once handled real outdoor swings is described in [why circulation still expects temperature contrast](https://research.betterhealthfacts.com/articles/why-your-circulation-still-expects-temperature-contrast/). Indoor life does not remove the reflex. It just makes the first blast of outdoor air more obvious.

## When to Worry

Get prompt care if mottling comes with chest pain, sudden shortness of breath, a cold pale limb, confusion, or a high fever and feeling very unwell. Those are not ordinary cold hands.

Book a routine visit if any of these keep happening:

- The net is still there after the skin has been warm for half an hour or more.
- The pattern is broken, branched, and irregular rather than a soft lace, especially if it is new.
- You also get finger ulcers, fingertip pits, hard skin on the fingers, or joint swelling.
- Color change is limited to one arm or one leg, or it arrives with pain and weakness.
- Mottling started with a new medicine.
- You have a history of clots, or fingers that turn stark white and stay numb in ordinary cool air.
- The hands are blotchy and you are pregnant with headache, swelling, or visual change. That needs same-day advice, not a watch-and-wait.

Painless lace that leaves when you warm up, on both hands, for years, is the everyday version.

## Myths vs Facts

Myth: Blotchy hands in the cold mean your circulation is blocked.
Fact: The net is open veins under tightened arterioles. Blood is moving. A blocked artery makes one limb pale, painful, and cold in a different way.

Myth: Blue patches mean you are low on oxygen.
Fact: Skin color in a cold net is local vessel tone. Lip and tongue color, and how you feel, tell you more about oxygen than the back of a cold hand.

Myth: Only people with Raynaud get this.
Fact: Most fair-skinned people can marble in cool air. Raynaud is a sharper, more symptomatic finger event.

Myth: You should rub the hands hard or shock them with hot water.
Fact: Hard rubbing and sudden heat on numb-cold skin can irritate or burn. Gradual warmth is safer.

## How to Manage It

Warm the core and the hands together. A layer on the trunk often clears the net faster than gloves alone, because trunk cooling is what keeps the hand vessels shut. Put gloves on before you go out, not after the hands are already marbled. Mittens beat finger gloves if the day is truly cold.

Dry your hands before wind hits them. Pocket the hands on the walk to the car. Wiggle the fingers and open and close the fists so muscle work pulls a little blood through. At home, warm water that feels comfortable on an elbow is warm enough. Skip the hottest tap.

If you smoke, this is one more reason the hands look worse in winter. Keep caffeine and decongestants modest on days you already run cold. A plain moisturizer helps if cold air also cracks the skin, but it will not stop the color change.

People who also get true white-finger attacks should mention that pattern to a clinician. Hand warming still helps, and some are offered calcium-channel treatment after a proper diagnosis. Mottling alone rarely needs a drug.

## When to See a Doctor

See a clinician if the pattern no longer fades with warmth, if it is new and broken rather than lacy, if sores appear on the fingers, or if one limb behaves differently from the other. Bring a photo taken in the cold and another taken after warming. That pair answers more questions than a description. Mention new medicines, smoking, and any history of clots or autoimmune disease.

If the only story is a winter lace that leaves in a warm room, you can treat it as a normal skin response and spend the energy on gloves and a warmer core.

## FAQs

**Is the blue color dangerous?**
Not when it is a soft net on cool skin that clears with warmth and you feel well. Persistent blue in a warm room, or blue with pain and a cold limb, needs a check.

**Why do my child’s hands look more marbled than mine?**
Infants and young children have immature vasomotor control and often less pigment hiding the venous net. It is common in a cool room and should fade when they are warm and well.

**Can anxiety cause the same look?**
Yes. Sympathetic nerves that fire with stress close the same hand vessels. A cool room plus nervousness is enough for a temporary net.

**Does mottling mean I have Raynaud?**
Only if you also get episodic, sharply bordered white or blue fingers with numbness, then a red throb on rewarming. A lace on the back of the hand is a different, usually harmless pattern.

**Will it get worse with age?**
Thin skin and fair skin can make the net easier to see. The reflex itself is not a disease that progresses. New mottling that stays after warming is the change that matters.

**Should I worry if only my knees and thighs do this?**
Thighs and knees often show physiologic livedo in cool air, especially in fair skin. The same rule applies: it should fade when you are warm.

## Conclusion

Blotchy, net-like hands in the cold are usually your skin doing a heat-saving job. Arterioles tighten, the surface venous net shows through, and the marble fades once the core and the hands warm up. It is a different picture from sharply white fingers, from sores, and from a broken pattern that never leaves. Gloves before you step out, a warmer trunk, and a photo if the net stops fading are enough for almost everyone who notices it on a winter morning.

<p>You stand up after a long meeting or a drive and your lower back feels stiff, as if the joints need a moment to unlock. A few steps later the sensation eases. That stiffness is often your facet joints reminding you they were never designed for hours of near-immobility.</p>

<p>Facet joints (also called zygapophyseal joints) are the paired synovial joints at the back of each vertebra. They guide and limit spinal motion while sharing load with the discs. Like any synovial joint, their cartilage depends on movement to stay nourished. In a life of frequent position changes, walking, and occasional deeper bends, they received that motion throughout the day. In a life of chairs, desks, and screens, they often do not.</p>

<h2>What the Facet Joints Actually Do</h2>

<p>Each spinal segment has two facet joints. Their orientation differs by region: more sagittal in the lumbar spine so they favor flexion and extension, more coronal higher up. The joint surfaces are covered in hyaline cartilage and enclosed by a capsule lined with synovium. Synovial fluid both lubricates the surfaces and supplies nutrients to the cartilage, which has no blood supply of its own.</p>

<p>Motion is the pump. Small glides and compressions squeeze fluid in and out of the cartilage matrix. Prolonged static postures reduce that exchange. Research on spinal loading, including work by Michael Adams and colleagues on facet and disc mechanics, shows that flexed sitting increases pressure on the posterior elements and can alter facet joint loading compared with upright walking. Nikolai Bogduk’s detailed anatomical descriptions of the lumbar facets emphasize how tightly their orientation and capsule design are matched to repeated, controlled motion rather than fixed postures.</p>

<h2>The Ancestral Pattern They Still Expect</h2>

<p>For most of human history the spine spent the day in motion. Walking produced continuous small rotations and side bends. Squatting, kneeling, and lifting involved deeper flexion followed by extension. Even rest involved frequent shifts. The facet joints experienced regular loading and unloading, and the cartilage received regular fluid exchange.</p>

<p>Modern chairs reverse much of that pattern. The lumbar spine often sits in slight flexion for hours. The facets remain in a relatively fixed position, synovial fluid circulation slows, and the capsule can stiffen. When you finally stand, the joints must re-establish their normal glide under load. That transition is when many people notice the familiar catch or stiffness. Related systems feel the same mismatch: the intervertebral discs also rely on walking and nightly unloading, and the natural lumbar curve is flattened by many chair designs.</p>

<h2>Common Everyday Triggers</h2>

<p>Long car trips, desk days, and soft couches that let the lower back round are the most frequent culprits. Working on a laptop in bed or on a sofa often places the facets in sustained flexion. Even “good” posture held rigidly for hours can reduce the small movements the joints need. Cold environments can make the stiffness more noticeable because synovial fluid viscosity rises slightly when tissue temperature drops.</p>

<p>Less commonly, a sudden twist while lifting, or sleeping in a twisted position, can irritate a facet joint and produce sharper, more localized pain that eases with gentle motion. True facet-mediated pain is usually mechanical: worse with extension or rotation, better with flexion or rest in a neutral position, and without the radiating leg symptoms of nerve root compression.</p>

<h2>When the Stiffness Is Worth Checking</h2>

<p>Occasional stiffness after sitting that resolves with a short walk is common and usually benign. See a clinician if the pain is persistent, wakes you at night, travels below the knee, is accompanied by numbness or weakness, or follows a significant trauma. Unexplained weight loss, fever, or a history of cancer also warrant prompt evaluation. Most mechanical facet-related discomfort does not require imaging on the first visit; a careful history and examination are usually sufficient to guide initial management.</p>

<h2>Myths Versus What the Joints Actually Need</h2>

<p><strong>Myth:</strong> Sitting is rest for the back.<br>
<strong>Fact:</strong> Sustained sitting reduces the fluid exchange the facet cartilage relies on. Short bouts of walking are closer to the stimulus these joints expect.</p>

<p><strong>Myth:</strong> A perfectly still, “correct” posture is protective.<br>
<strong>Fact:</strong> The joints need frequent small changes in position. Rigid holding can be as limiting as slouching.</p>

<p><strong>Myth:</strong> Cracking or popping in the lower back means damage.<br>
<strong>Fact:</strong> Occasional cavitation in a synovial joint is usually harmless gas release. Painful, repetitive catching is more relevant than the sound itself.</p>

<h2>How to Give the Facets the Motion They Expect</h2>

<p>The simplest intervention is interrupting static sitting. Stand and walk for two to three minutes every 30–45 minutes. Even gentle lumbar extensions—standing and placing your hands on your hips and leaning slightly backward—can restore facet glide after prolonged flexion.</p>

<p>Include some rotation and side-bending in your day. Thoracic rotations while seated or kneeling, or a slow cat-cow on the floor, move the facets through ranges they rarely see at a desk. If you drive long distances, use rest stops to walk rather than simply stretching in place. Strengthening the deep spinal stabilizers and hip extensors supports the facets under load, but the primary need is still frequent motion rather than more sitting with better posture.</p>

<h2>Frequently Asked Questions</h2>

<p><strong>Is lower-back stiffness after sitting always the facet joints?</strong><br>
Not always. Discs, muscles, and ligaments can contribute. Facet-related stiffness often feels localized to one side of the lower back and eases once you begin walking.</p>

<p><strong>Do facet joints wear out with age?</strong><br>
Some cartilage changes are common with age, yet many people with imaging findings of facet arthropathy have no pain. Motion and load management appear more important than chronological age alone.</p>

<p><strong>Should I avoid extension if my back feels stiff?</strong><br>
Gentle, pain-free extension after sitting is usually helpful. Sharp pain with extension, especially if it radiates, is a reason to stop and seek assessment.</p>

<p><strong>Can a standing desk solve this?</strong><br>
Standing changes the load, but standing still for hours creates its own static demand. Alternating between sitting, standing, and short walks is closer to the varied pattern the joints expect.</p>

<p><strong>Is cracking my back harmful to the facets?</strong><br>
Occasional self-manipulation that does not cause pain is unlikely to damage healthy facets. Forced or painful twisting, especially under load, is best avoided.</p>

<h2>A Small Daily Adjustment</h2>

<p>Your facet joints are not fragile; they are simply under-stimulated by long periods of stillness. The stiffness you feel when you stand is often the sensation of cartilage and capsule that have been waiting for the small glides and extensions a walking day once supplied automatically. Frequent, low-effort movement—standing, a short walk, a gentle backward lean—restores the fluid exchange and joint motion they still expect. Over weeks, that habit tends to reduce the end-of-day catch more reliably than any single stretch or posture cue.</p>

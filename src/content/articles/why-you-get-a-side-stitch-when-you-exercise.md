<p>You are ten minutes into an easy run, a pickup game, or a brisk walk after lunch when a sharp, grabbing pain appears just under the ribs. Breathing suddenly feels like it has a hinge. You slow down, press the spot, and wonder whether you should be worried.</p>

<p>Most of the time you should not. A side stitch — clinicians often call it exercise-related transient abdominal pain — is one of the most common complaints in running and field sports. It is annoying, it can stop a workout, and it is rarely a sign that something in the abdomen is breaking. It is a mechanical and breathing problem showing up at speed.</p>

<h2>What a Side Stitch Feels Like</h2>

<p>The classic stitch is a sharp or cramping pain in a narrow band, usually on the right side just below the ribcage, sometimes on the left, occasionally closer to the midline. It often worsens with deeper breaths and eases within minutes of slowing or stopping. Some people also feel a dull ache in the shoulder tip on the same side.</p>

<p>It is different from a muscle strain you can poke for days, and different from the spreading pressure of heart-related pain. It arrives with movement and breathing. It leaves when the bounce and the breath settle.</p>

<h2>What Is Actually Going On</h2>

<p>There is no single agreed mechanism, which is why stitches feel mysterious. Researchers have studied several overlapping ideas, and more than one can be true in the same person on different days.</p>

<p><strong>The diaphragm is working hard while it is being jostled.</strong> Running and similar sports bounce the organs. The diaphragm — the dome of muscle under the lungs that still expects a fuller job than <a href="/the-breath-that-was-never-meant-for-seated-life">seated life</a> usually gives it — has to keep a rhythm while the liver and stomach tug on the ligaments that help suspend them. A sudden demand for bigger breaths on an unprepared muscle can feel like a cramp in the lower chest wall.</p>

<p><strong>The lining of the abdomen can be irritated by motion.</strong> The parietal peritoneum is a sensitive layer. Repeated up-and-down movement, especially after a meal when the stomach is fuller, may pull on that lining enough to create a sharp, localized grab.</p>

<p><strong>Breathing pattern matters as much as fitness.</strong> Shallow, rushed chest breathing leaves the lower ribs and diaphragm less coordinated. Many people stitch more when they start faster than their breathing has warmed up, or when they run soon after eating or drinking a large volume.</p>

<p><strong>Trunk control is part of the story.</strong> A core that still expects <a href="/why-your-core-still-expects-you-to-carry-things">carrying and upright work</a> helps stabilize the torso so organs and the diaphragm are not sloshing unsupported. A sudden return to bouncing after weeks of sitting can outrun that support.</p>

<h2>Common Triggers</h2>

<ul>
  <li>Starting a run or game too fast without a gradual warm-up</li>
  <li>Eating a large meal, especially fatty or high-fiber, within about two hours</li>
  <li>Gulping a big drink right before you go</li>
  <li>Running in cold air with tight, high shoulders and a held breath</li>
  <li>Poor fitness for the pace you chose that day — stitches are more common in newer runners, but fit people get them too</li>
  <li>Side-bending sports or carrying a bag on one side before you run</li>
</ul>

<h2>Hidden Triggers</h2>

<ul>
  <li>Talking in long sentences while climbing a hill</li>
  <li>A tight belt, waistband, or hydration pack pressing the lower ribs</li>
  <li>Afternoon workouts after a heavy desk lunch, when bloating already occupies space under the diaphragm</li>
  <li>Only breathing on one foot-strike pattern for miles (some runners stitch when exhaling always on the same side)</li>
  <li>Returning to running after illness, jet lag, or a week of pure sitting</li>
</ul>

<h2>Less Common but Serious</h2>

<p>A stitch that is truly only a stitch fades with rest and does not come with warning signs. Pain that radiates into the chest, jaw, or left arm, pain with fainting or unusual shortness of breath, vomiting, fever, or a rigid abdomen is not a stitch. Neither is pain that stays severe for hours after you stop moving.</p>

<p>People with known gallstones, ulcers, or reflux can have those conditions unmasked by bouncing after a meal. Women can occasionally feel ovulation or ovarian-cyst discomfort more clearly when running. Those stories deserve a medical visit if they repeat.</p>

<h2>When to Worry</h2>

<ul>
  <li>Pain with chest pressure, dizziness, or breathlessness out of proportion to the effort</li>
  <li>Pain that does not ease within 15–20 minutes of stopping</li>
  <li>Fever, vomiting, or a swollen, tender abdomen</li>
  <li>A stitch that is always in the same spot and getting worse over weeks</li>
  <li>Black stools, unexplained weight loss, or pain that wakes you at night</li>
</ul>

<h2>Myths vs Facts</h2>

<p><strong>Myth:</strong> A stitch means your spleen or liver is “enlarging with blood.”</p>
<p><strong>Fact:</strong> That old explanation does not match how those organs work during ordinary exercise. Ligament tug, peritoneal irritation, and diaphragm fatigue are better supported.</p>

<p><strong>Myth:</strong> Only beginners get stitches.</p>
<p><strong>Fact:</strong> They are more common when people are new or under-conditioned for the pace, but experienced athletes get them after a big meal, a cold start, or a clumsy breathing day.</p>

<p><strong>Myth:</strong> You must stop completely and wait it out on the sidewalk.</p>
<p><strong>Fact:</strong> Slowing, dropping the arms, exhaling fully, and walking a minute often lets you resume. Stopping is fine. It is not mandatory if the pain is the typical sharp, breath-linked grab.</p>

<p><strong>Myth:</strong> Sports drinks or gels cause stitches, so you should never fuel.</p>
<p><strong>Fact:</strong> A large volume in the stomach can contribute. Small, practiced sips are a different stimulus than a bottle chugged at the start line.</p>

<h2>How to Prevent and Ease a Stitch</h2>

<p><strong>Warm the breath before you warm the ego.</strong> Two to five minutes of easy walking, then a slow jog, gives the diaphragm time to match the bounce. The first hard interval is a common stitch window.</p>

<p><strong>Leave a gap after bigger meals.</strong> Many people do well waiting 90–120 minutes after a full plate. A small snack is usually kinder than a feast plus an immediate run — a theme that also shows up in afternoon <a href="/why-you-feel-bloated-every-afternoon">bloating</a>.</p>

<p><strong>Practice a longer exhale.</strong> When the stitch appears, slow down and breathe out fully, as if fogging a mirror, then let the inhale drop low into the ribs. Tight, high shoulders make the cramp easier to hold.</p>

<p><strong>Use the opposite arm and a slight bend.</strong> Lightly pressing the sore spot while bending a little toward that side, then standing tall again, helps some people. Others do better reaching the arm on the stitch side overhead and breathing into the stretch. Use what eases the grab, not a ritual.</p>

<p><strong>Change the foot-breath link.</strong> If you always exhale when the same foot lands, try a few cycles that break the pattern. The evidence is mixed, but the experiment is free.</p>

<p><strong>Train the trunk without turning it into a brace.</strong> Easy carries, tall walking, and gentle rotation give the torso the support bouncing sports use. A rigid, held stomach is not the goal. A responsive midsection is.</p>

<p><strong>Check the waistband.</strong> Anything that pins the lower ribs during deep breaths is an easy fix.</p>

<h2>When to See a Doctor</h2>

<p>See a clinician if stitches are new and severe, always one-sided and lingering, or paired with reflux that is getting worse, unexplained fatigue, or chest symptoms. A primary-care visit can sort ordinary exercise pain from gallbladder, stomach, lung, or heart causes. Most young, otherwise well people with a classic fading stitch never need imaging.</p>

<h2>FAQs</h2>

<p><strong>Why is it usually on the right?</strong><br />The liver is the large organ under the right ribs, so ligament tug on that side may be more noticeable. Left-sided stitches happen too and are still usually benign when they behave like a typical stitch.</p>

<p><strong>Can children get stitches?</strong><br />Yes, especially in running games after a quick lunch. The same rules help: slower start, less volume in the stomach, and full exhales.</p>

<p><strong>Does swimming cause a different stitch?</strong><br />Swimmers can feel a similar grab, often tied to breathing pattern and rotation rather than ground impact. The management is still rhythm and not starting at race pace from the first length.</p>

<p><strong>Will core workouts stop stitches forever?</strong><br />They can reduce how often the torso feels unsupported. They will not cancel a huge pre-run meal or a sprint from a cold start.</p>

<p><strong>Is a stitch the same as a cramp in the side overnight?</strong><br />No. Night pain, pain at rest, or pain after a blow to the ribs belongs in a different conversation with a clinician.</p>

<h2>Conclusion</h2>

<p>A side stitch is your breathing muscle and your bouncing insides arguing about timing. It is common, usually brief, and very responsive to a slower start, a little space after meals, and a longer exhale.</p>

<p>Treat the first grab as information, not a verdict on your fitness. Ease the pace, let the ribs move, and try again. Most bodies remember how to run once the diaphragm is invited into the workout instead of startled by it.</p>

You are about to speak, and your tongue sticks to the roof of your mouth. Water is right there. You drank some. The dryness still arrives with the nerves, then fades once the meeting ends. It can feel like you forgot to hydrate, or like something is wrong with the glands.

For most people it is neither. Resting saliva is a parasympathetic habit. Alertness turns that habit down, thickens what is left, and often adds mouth breathing that dries the film even faster. The cotton feeling is real. The story behind it is usually a short nervous-system shift, not a failing mouth.

## What This Feels Like

The usual pattern is timed to the moment. The mouth feels sticky before a presentation, a phone call, a test, or a difficult conversation. Speech sounds less smooth. Swallowing feels like a dry click. Lips may catch. A sip helps for a minute, then the film thins again if the nerves stay up.

Once the pressure drops, moisture often returns within minutes. Some people also notice a metallic edge, a tight throat, or the need to clear the voice. Those travel with the same arousal. They are not a separate illness if they leave with the situation.

A smaller group stays dry all day, wakes dry, or struggles with crackers and bread even on calm days. That pattern deserves a different look. Nervous dryness is a spike. Ongoing dryness is a baseline.

## Why Alertness Turns Saliva Down

Walter Cannon, writing on bodily changes in fear and rage, treated a dry mouth as part of the emergency pattern. Digestion can wait. Attention, heart rate, and muscle readiness cannot. Saliva is digestion’s opening move, so the resting flow is an easy thing to pause.

The hardware is autonomic. Watery saliva is driven mainly by parasympathetic nerves using acetylcholine at the glands. Jörgen Ekström and earlier physiologists such as Nils Emmelin mapped that drive: it keeps a slow unstimulated flow even when you are not eating. Sympathetic traffic, the alertness channel, changes the protein mix and can make the remaining saliva feel thicker and stringier. In a scare, parasympathetic tone often falls at the same time, so the net result is less volume, not a flood.

Colin Dawes measured ordinary resting flow in adults at roughly a third of a milliliter a minute, with wide personal differences. Subjective dryness tends to appear when unstimulated flow drops toward about a tenth of a milliliter a minute, a threshold also emphasized in xerostomia work by Leo Sreebny. You do not need the glands to stop. You need the film on the tongue and palate to thin. Nervousness can do that quickly.

Gordon Proctor’s reviews of salivary control add a second layer. What little saliva remains under stress is often richer in proteins. It coats less evenly. The mouth feels dry even when a swab would still find some moisture. That mismatch between “feels dry” and “glands still working” is common in short anxiety spikes.

## The Extra Drying You Do Yourself

Alertness changes behavior around the mouth. People talk faster, swallow less often, and breathe through the mouth. Each of those steals water from a film that was already thinner.

Mouth breathing is the quiet multiplier. Nasal air is warmed and wetted. Oral air is not. A few minutes of open-mouth breathing before you speak can evaporate the surface layer that chewing and quiet nasal breathing would have protected. Jos Bosch and colleagues, studying saliva under social stress, have shown that flow and composition shift with the task, not only with how much water you drank that morning. A full glass does not override a parasympathetic pause.

Chewing would normally rescue the film. Mastication is one of the strongest everyday switches for watery saliva, which is why a dry mouth at rest can still feel normal at lunch. A nervous moment rarely includes chewing. The glands are waiting for a meal signal that never comes.

If palm sweat shows up in the same minute, that is the sister reflex, not a contradiction. Sole and palm glands fire with alertness. Salivary glands quiet. Same arousal, opposite jobs. The piece on [sweaty palms when you are nervous](https://research.betterhealthfacts.com/articles/is-it-normal-to-get-sweaty-palms-when-youre-nervous/) walks through that eccrine side of the same alarm.

## Common Triggers That Make It Worse

A few ordinary stacks turn a brief dry spell into an obvious one.

- Caffeine and a skipped breakfast. Alertness is already high, and there has been no chew to prime the glands.
- Antihistamines, some antidepressants, bladder medicines, and other anticholinergic drugs. They block the same acetylcholine signal the glands use. Nerves on top of that block feel extreme.
- Mouth breathing from a stuffy nose, a mask, or talking over noise.
- Dry indoor air. The film evaporates faster once flow dips. That is a different problem from all-night mouth breathing, covered in [waking up with a dry mouth](https://research.betterhealthfacts.com/articles/why-you-wake-up-with-a-dry-mouth/).
- Alcohol the night before, or a diuretic day, so the baseline film was already thin.
- Constant sipping of acidic or caffeinated drinks instead of a real chew. The mouth stays busy and never gets a salivary reset. [Why saliva still expects real chewing](https://research.betterhealthfacts.com/articles/why-your-saliva-still-expects-real-chewing/) is the longer version of that mismatch.

Public speaking is the classic test because it combines all of them: sympathetic surge, open mouth, fast airflow, and no chewing.

## Less Common but Serious Causes

Dryness that ignores the calendar of your nerves is a different category.

Medications are the most common lasting cause in adults. A new pill and a new dry mouth in the same fortnight is a clue worth telling a clinician, not a character flaw.

Sjögren’s pattern is dryness of mouth and eyes that persists on calm days, often with swollen glands, rampant new cavities, or trouble swallowing dry food. It is autoimmune, not stage fright.

Uncontrolled high blood sugar pulls water into urine and leaves the mouth dry with unusual thirst. Nerve damage later can affect the glands too. Night thirst plus frequent urination is the flag, not a single pre-meeting cotton mouth.

Blocked saliva flow from a stone or infection tends to be one-sided, painful at meals, and sometimes swollen under the jaw. Anxiety dryness is both sides and painless.

True dehydration dries more than the mouth. Urine is dark, the head aches, and the skin and energy drop with it. A nervous dry mouth with pale urine is not that picture.

## When to Worry

Most nervous dryness can be watched. Call or book a visit if any of these show up.

- Dryness on ordinary calm days, lasting weeks, not just before stressful moments.
- Difficulty swallowing dry food, new cavities, or cracked corners of the mouth that do not settle.
- Dry eyes, joint aches, or swollen saliva glands alongside the mouth symptom.
- One-sided pain or swelling when you eat.
- Extreme thirst, weight loss, or waking to urinate much more than usual.
- Dry mouth that began with a new medicine and is interfering with eating or speech.

Sudden facial weakness, trouble speaking that is not just a sticky tongue, or one-sided numbness is an emergency pattern. A dry mouth alone is not.

## Myths vs Facts

Myth: A dry mouth under stress means you are dehydrated.
Fact: You can be well hydrated and still stop resting flow. The switch is neural. A sip wets the surface. It does not restart the parasympathetic drive by itself.

Myth: If you can still spit, the dryness is imaginary.
Fact: Feeling dry tracks the thin surface film, not the total daily volume. Thick, protein-rich saliva feels dry. Dawes and later clinical work separated flow rate from the sensation.

Myth: Stress dryness means the glands are failing.
Fact: The same glands usually pour during a meal. Failure looks like dryness at meals too, or dryness that never lifts.

Myth: Only anxious people get this.
Fact: Any sharp alertness can do it: a near miss in traffic, a hard question, cold-call nerves. Trait anxiety makes it more often. It does not invent the reflex.

## How to Manage It

Treat the minute, then the baseline.

Before you speak, nasal breathing for a minute or two beats another huge gulp. Slow exhales tell the parasympathetic side it can return. A small sip, held and swished, wets the palate better than chugging.

A brief chew is the strongest ordinary switch. Sugar-free gum or a small piece of something you actually chew, used before the moment rather than during a sentence, raises watery flow. That is the meal signal the glands still expect.

Skip the last coffee if you already know this talk dries you out. Caffeine adds arousal and a mild diuretic push. Alcohol the night before does the same to the morning film.

If a medicine is the amplifier, do not stop it on your own. Ask whether the dose, the timing, or an alternative is realistic. Saliva substitutes and a bedroom humidifier help the baseline. They do not fix a ten-minute surge, but they keep the starting film thicker.

Practice the first line out loud once. The dry click is worse when the mouth has been closed and still. A real swallow and a real phrase recruit the muscles that spread saliva.

## When to See a Doctor

See a clinician if dryness has become the daily background, not the pre-talk spike. Bring a list of medicines, including antihistamines and sleep aids. Mention eyes, joints, and whether meals still produce saliva. Dental checks matter if cavities are suddenly new. Blood sugar is worth a look when thirst and urination changed too.

A single sticky mouth before a known stressor, gone afterward, does not need a workup.

## FAQs

### Why does water only help for a minute?

Water wets the surface. It is not saliva, so it has little of the mucus that holds a film in place. If the glands are still paused, the next breaths evaporate the sip. Chewing or the end of the stressor restarts flow. Sipping again is fine. It is a surface fix.

### Can anxiety cause a dry mouth even when I do not feel that scared?

Yes. Anticipation, a deadline, or a low-grade brace can lower parasympathetic tone before you would call it panic. Bosch’s stress studies and older autonomic work both show salivary shifts with social and cognitive load, not only with obvious fear. If it vanishes on a quiet day, that pattern still fits.

### Does a nervous dry mouth damage teeth?

A short spike does not. Saliva that stays low for hours, day after day, matters more, because saliva buffers acid and clears food. If dryness is only around stressful events, cavity risk barely moves. If it is constant, mention it at a dental visit.

### Why does my voice crack at the same time?

The vocal folds like a wet surface. Less saliva, more mouth breathing, and a tight throat from the same arousal make the voice sound rough or unreliable. It usually clears with the moisture. Lasting hoarseness is a separate issue.

### Should I worry if only one side feels dry?

Nervous dryness is almost always both sides. One-sided dryness with mealtime pain or a swelling under the jaw can be a stone or a blocked duct. That is worth a clinical look even if you were also nervous.

### Is this the same as morning dry mouth?

No. Morning dryness is mostly overnight mouth breathing and a long pause in swallowing. Nervous dryness is a daytime autonomic dip tied to alertness. They can stack, especially if you already breathe through your mouth, but they are different switches.

## Conclusion

A mouth that goes dry when you are nervous is a normal pause in resting saliva, plus a little extra evaporation from how you breathe and speak. Cannon’s emergency pattern, Dawes’s flow numbers, and later work on stress and glands all point the same way: the system is briefly uninterested in digestion. Sip, breathe through your nose, chew something small before the moment, and expect the film to return when the moment ends. Dryness that stays after the nerves leave is the pattern worth checking. The spike that leaves with them is the mouth doing an old job on a modern timetable.

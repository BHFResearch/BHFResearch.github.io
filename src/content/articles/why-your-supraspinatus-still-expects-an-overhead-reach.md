<p>The ache shows up on a high cupboard, not in the gym. You reach for a box, the outer shoulder catches, and the arm wants to shrug instead of lift. It is often the supraspinatus, a small rotator-cuff muscle that still expects a short, clean overhead reach. Most modern days never give it one. The arm stays in front of the body, the shoulder blade sits forward, and the tendon slides under a bony roof it was not meant to live against.</p>

<h2>What This Shoulder Catch Feels Like</h2>

<p>People describe a pinch at the top or outer shoulder when the arm goes above shoulder height, especially with the thumb down. A dull ache can linger after painting a ceiling, loading a suitcase into an overhead bin, or sleeping on that side. The first few degrees of lifting a light bag may feel weak, then stronger once the arm is partway up. A click without pain is common and usually harmless. Pain that stops the lift, night pain that wakes you, or true weakness holding a cup out to the side is a different story.</p>

<h2>What the Tendon Was Built to Do</h2>

<p>The supraspinatus sits in a groove on the back of the shoulder blade and sends a flat tendon across the top of the ball of the humerus, onto the greater tubercle. Charles Neer later made the passage under the acromion famous, but the job was mapped earlier. Emanuel Kaplan and then Verne Inman described the force couple: the deltoid lifts the arm, and the supraspinatus, with the rest of the cuff, keeps the ball centered in the socket so it does not ride up into the roof.</p>

<p>It is busiest at the start of abduction, roughly the first fifteen to thirty degrees, and it keeps helping through elevation. Ernest Codman, in the 1930s, called the insertion zone near the tubercle the critical zone, because that is where the tendon frays. Ian Macnab and later Richard Rathbun linked that zone to relatively poor blood flow when the arm hangs. Subsequent work, including studies by Brooks and colleagues, found the blood supply less empty than the old story claimed. Compression and repeated load explain more of the fraying than a permanently bloodless patch.</p>

<p>The reach itself is old. A mobile human shoulder still throws, hangs, and lifts food or a child overhead. The tendon expects that arc a few times a day, with the shoulder blade rotating up so the roof lifts out of the way. It does not expect eight hours of a mouse, then one awkward grab for a jar.</p>

<h2>Why a Desk Arm Is the Wrong Shape</h2>

<p>Typing holds the humerus slightly forward and turned in. The shoulder blade drifts into a protracted, downwardly rotated rest. Paula Ludewig’s scapular work, and later summaries by W. Ben Kibler, showed that if the blade does not upwardly rotate and posteriorly tilt as the arm rises, the space under the acromion narrows. The supraspinatus tendon is the structure in that space.</p>

<p>Neer’s 1972 impingement account blamed the anterior acromion and the coracoacromial ligament. Louis Bigliani added acromion shape as a risk. Those bony stories are incomplete on their own. Many people with a hooked acromion never hurt, and many painful shoulders have a normal roof. Stephen Burkhart’s cable-and-crescent model treats the tendon as a suspension bridge: the thick cable can carry load even if the thinner crescent thins with age. Age-related fraying is common on scans in people who feel fine. The mismatch is the combination — a quiet cuff, a blade that does not clear the roof, and a sudden overhead demand.</p>

<h2>Hidden Triggers</h2>

<ul>
  <li>Reaching into a back seat or overhead bin with the palm down and the shoulder shrugged.</li>
  <li>Sleeping on the sore side with the arm tucked under the pillow, so the tendon stays compressed for hours.</li>
  <li>A sudden weekend of painting, pruning, or swimming after months of desk work.</li>
  <li>A heavy bag on one shoulder that hikes the blade and shortens the upper trapezius while the cuff stays quiet.</li>
  <li>Keyboard and phone postures that keep the arm internally rotated, so the greater tubercle meets the roof earlier in the lift.</li>
  <li>A low screen that pulls the head forward and rounds the upper back, leaving less room for the blade to tilt back.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Less Common but Serious Causes</h2>

<p>A partial or full-thickness tear can follow a fall on an outstretched arm, or it can accumulate in older tendons. Frank Jobe’s exam work, and later empty-can and drop-arm tests used by Lori Michener and others, tries to separate a painful tendon from a tendon that cannot hold the arm. Calcific deposits can inflame the same tendon and cause severe night pain. A pinched nerve in the neck can mimic outer-shoulder pain and may add tingling into the hand. True weakness, swelling, fever, or pain after a clear injury is not a simple desk mismatch.</p>

<h2>When to Worry</h2>

<ul>
  <li>You cannot lift the arm after a fall, or it drops when you try to hold it out.</li>
  <li>Night pain wakes you most nights and does not ease when you change position.</li>
  <li>Weakness is obvious compared with the other arm, not just a pinch at the top of the reach.</li>
  <li>Pain travels with numbness or tingling below the elbow.</li>
  <li>The shoulder is hot, swollen, or followed a fever.</li>
  <li>The catch has lasted more than a few weeks despite easier reaching and simple movement.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Myths vs Facts</h2>

<ul>
  <li><strong>Myth:</strong> A pinch overhead means the tendon is torn.<br/><strong>Fact:</strong> Most first-time catches are compression and a deconditioned cuff. Tears are more likely after injury, with clear weakness, or in older shoulders that have ached for months.</li>
  <li><strong>Myth:</strong> You should never lift the arm overhead again.<br/><strong>Fact:</strong> Avoiding the reach is how the muscle loses the job it was built for. The useful change is a better blade position and a gradual return, not a permanent ban.</li>
  <li><strong>Myth:</strong> A hooked bone is the whole explanation.<br/><strong>Fact:</strong> Neer and Bigliani described the roof. Ludewig, Kibler, and Burkhart showed that motion, load sharing, and age-related tendon change matter at least as much.</li>
  <li><strong>Myth:</strong> Ice and rest for two weeks fix the pattern.<br/><strong>Fact:</strong> Calm the flare, then give the tendon the short overhead arcs and scapular rotation it stopped getting.</li>
</ul>

<h2>How to Give It the Reach It Expects</h2>

<p>Set the blade before you chase the jar. Gently draw the shoulder blade down and around the ribs, then reach with the thumb pointing up, as if offering a cup, rather than palm-down. A few slow reaches to a shelf, a branch, or a clothesline during the day are closer to the old pattern than one heroic set of presses.</p>

<p>Wall slides help the upward rotation the tendon needs: forearms on a wall, slide up only as far as the pinch allows, and come down under control. Light side-lying or standing lifts in the scapular plane, thumb up, rebuild the early-abduction job Inman described, without jamming the arm straight out to the side. Hanging from a bar is optional and only if the shoulder is calm. A short, supported hang is discussed with the blade’s own story in <a href="https://research.betterhealthfacts.com/articles/why-your-scapulae-still-expect-you-to-hang/">why the shoulder blades still expect you to hang</a>.</p>

<p>During a flare, keep the arm moving below the pinch, sleep slightly propped or on the other side, and skip sudden overhead painting. Progress by range first, then by light load. A tendon that has been quiet for months does not want a weekend of max effort.</p>

<h2>When to See a Doctor</h2>

<p>See a clinician if weakness, night pain, or a post-fall loss of lift is present, or if a simple pinch has not settled in a few weeks. An exam often tells more than an early scan. Imaging is useful when a tear, calcification, or a neck source is plausible. Physical therapy that restores scapular upward rotation and gradual cuff loading is the usual first path for an uncomplicated catch. Injections and surgery are for tendons that stay weak or keep failing despite a fair trial of load.</p>

<h2>FAQs</h2>

<h3>Is a click when I lift my arm the supraspinatus?</h3>
<p>Often it is a tendon or bursa flicking under the roof, or a harmless joint cavitation. A painless click can be left alone. A click with a pinch at the top of the reach fits the supraspinatus passage better than a deep joint problem.</p>

<h3>Why does it hurt more with my thumb down?</h3>
<p>Internal rotation swings the greater tubercle toward the acromion earlier in the lift, so the tendon has less room. Thumb-up, in the plane of the blade, is the friendlier version of the same reach.</p>

<h3>Can sitting really bother a muscle on the back of the shoulder?</h3>
<p>Yes. The muscle is quiet, but the blade position from sitting decides how much roof the tendon meets the next time you reach. The mismatch is stillness plus a badly set overhead grab, not the chair pressing on the tendon itself.</p>

<h3>Should I stretch the shoulder harder if it feels tight?</h3>
<p>Aggressive overhead stretching into a pinch usually compresses the tendon more. Ease the upper back and the front of the shoulder, then practice a small pain-free reach with the blade set. Force is not the missing ingredient.</p>

<h3>Does a scan that says “tendinosis” mean I am injured?</h3>
<p>Not by itself. Age-related signal change in the supraspinatus is common in people without pain. Burkhart’s work is a reminder that a thinner crescent can still be carried by the cable. Symptoms, strength, and how you reach matter more than a single word on a report.</p>

<h3>How soon should overhead reaching feel normal again?</h3>
<p>A mild desk-related catch often eases over one to three weeks of better reaching and light loading. A tendon that has frayed for months can take longer. Sudden weakness or night pain that will not settle deserves a look rather than more waiting.</p>

<h2>Conclusion</h2>

<p>The supraspinatus still expects a brief overhead reach that centers the ball and lets the shoulder blade clear the roof. Desk life withholds that arc, then asks for it in a shrugged, palm-down grab. The pinch is usually the tendon meeting a space it no longer gets to practice. Give it small, thumb-up reaches through the week, and treat true weakness or night pain as a reason to get the shoulder examined.</p>

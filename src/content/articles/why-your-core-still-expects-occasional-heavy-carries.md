<p>You sit at a desk, stand at a counter, or walk a short distance with almost nothing in your hands. Hours later the middle of your body feels a little loose, a little stiff, or just vaguely tired even though you have not done anything that looks hard. That quiet under-use is common. Your core muscles evolved to do more than hold you upright for sitting; they were built to stiffen the trunk so force could move from legs to arms (or the other way) while you carried something useful.</p>

<p>Most people think of the core as the abs that show in a mirror. Biomechanically it is the whole cylinder around the spine and pelvis: the deep transverse abdominis, the obliques, the multifidus, the diaphragm, the pelvic floor, and the thicker outer layers that wrap the torso. Spine biomechanics researcher Stuart McGill has spent decades measuring how these muscles work. In real life they usually act to prevent unwanted motion rather than create large bending. When the cylinder stiffens, the hips and shoulders can generate power without the spine buckling. Carrying a load is one of the simplest ways that system gets the brief, purposeful stiffening it was designed for.</p>

<h2>What This Feels Like in Everyday Life</h2>

<p>The mismatch rarely announces itself with a dramatic injury. It shows up as a low-grade sense that your midsection is “switched off” after a long day of sitting, that ordinary bending feels a little less solid, or that you notice your lower back more when you finally pick something up. Some people describe a vague hollow feeling or a mild ache that improves once they start moving and carrying again. Others simply feel less stable on one foot or when they turn quickly.</p>

<p>None of this is proof that something is broken. It is the expected result of a trunk that almost never has to create and hold stiffness against an external load. Chair sitting and light walking with empty hands ask the core for almost nothing compared with the occasional heavy or asymmetric carry that was once ordinary.</p>

<h2>Why the Core Still Expects a Carry</h2>

<p>For most of human evolutionary history the trunk was a working linkage. People carried infants, tools, water, food, and firewood. The load was rarely perfectly balanced and rarely held for only a few seconds. The muscles around the spine had to increase stiffness so the pelvis stayed relatively steady while the legs stepped and the arms adjusted. That brief stiffening is exactly what McGill’s work describes as the primary job of the core: proximal stability so distal segments (arms and legs) can move with speed and control.</p>

<p>Modern environments removed most of those loads. Groceries arrive in a cart or a bag that weighs little. Children are often carried in seats or strollers rather than on a hip for long stretches. Work is done with hands on a keyboard instead of hands supporting a tool or a basket. The result is a core that spends most of its day in a low-demand state. Muscles that are rarely asked to stiffen against a real load become less efficient at creating that stiffness when it is finally needed. The same pattern appears in other tissues that evolved for load: tendons, fascia, and even the small deep stabilizers of the spine.</p>

<p>Research on hunter-gatherer and subsistence groups shows that even their “rest” often involved postures with more muscle activity than modern chair sitting. The Inactivity Mismatch Hypothesis (Raichlen, Pontzer and colleagues) notes that our physiology appears adapted to consistent low-level muscle activity throughout the day, not just to formal exercise sessions. Occasional carries are one simple way to restore a piece of that pattern without turning daily life into a workout.</p>

<h2>Common Modern Triggers</h2>

<ul>
<li>Long periods of sitting with no external load on the arms or trunk</li>
<li>Walking only with a light phone or empty hands</li>
<li>Relying on machines or carts for every object that once had to be carried</li>
<li>Sudden heavy lifts after weeks of almost no carrying (the classic “I moved a few boxes and my back complained”)</li>
<li>Asymmetric habits such as always carrying a bag on one shoulder</li>
</ul>

<p>These are not moral failures. They are the default settings of current environments. The body simply continues to expect the occasional demand it used to receive.</p>

<h2>Less Common but Serious Possibilities</h2>

<p>Persistent one-sided weakness, true numbness, or pain that radiates down a leg are not explained by simple under-use. Those patterns need evaluation for nerve involvement, disc issues, or other structural problems. Sudden severe pain after a specific lift, or any loss of bowel or bladder control, is an emergency and not part of ordinary core under-stimulation. For the large majority of people who simply feel a bit less solid after desk days, the everyday mismatch is the more likely explanation.</p>

<h2>Hidden Triggers That Amplify the Effect</h2>

<p>Poor sleep, high stress, and low overall daily movement make the under-used core feel worse. When the nervous system is already taxed, the small deep muscles that provide fine stiffness are less responsive. Very soft surfaces (deep couches, sagging mattresses) can also leave the trunk in a collapsed position for long periods, reducing even the mild postural demand that standing or sitting upright would provide. Related patterns appear in articles such as <a href="https://research.betterhealthfacts.com/articles/why-your-spine-still-expects-frequent-position-changes/">Why Your Spine Still Expects Frequent Position Changes</a> and <a href="https://research.betterhealthfacts.com/articles/why-your-hamstrings-still-expect-you-to-pick-things-up/">Why Your Hamstrings Still Expect You to Pick Things Up</a>.</p>

<h2>When to Worry</h2>

<p>Seek professional assessment if:</p>
<ul>
<li>Pain is sharp, progressive, or wakes you at night</li>
<li>You develop true weakness, numbness, or tingling in a leg</li>
<li>Pain follows a specific injury and does not settle within a few days</li>
<li>You have a history of cancer, unexplained weight loss, or recent infection alongside new back symptoms</li>
</ul>

<p>Mild stiffness or a sense of reduced stability after long sitting is common and usually improves with gradual reintroduction of ordinary carries and movement variety.</p>

<h2>Myths vs Facts</h2>

<p><strong>Myth:</strong> A strong core means endless sit-ups or planks.<br>
<strong>Fact:</strong> McGill’s research shows that the core’s main job is often to stiffen and prevent motion so force can be transferred safely. Endless flexion (sit-ups) can load discs repeatedly without teaching the stiffness pattern used in real carrying.</p>

<p><strong>Myth:</strong> If you walk every day your core is already getting everything it needs.<br>
<strong>Fact:</strong> Unloaded walking is excellent for many tissues, but it asks the trunk for far less stiffness than carrying even a modest asymmetric load. Both have value; they are not identical.</p>

<p><strong>Myth:</strong> Core work must be done in a gym with equipment.<br>
<strong>Fact:</strong> Everyday carries (grocery bags, a child on one hip for a short distance, a toolbox, a backpack with a reasonable load) provide the exact stimulus the system evolved to handle.</p>

<h2>How to Give the Core What It Still Expects</h2>

<p>You do not need a formal program. Simple, frequent, moderate carries restore the missing demand:</p>
<ul>
<li>Carry grocery bags in one hand or split between both hands for the walk from car to kitchen. Alternate sides across days.</li>
<li>Use a backpack with a modest load for short walks instead of a tiny cross-body bag.</li>
<li>When you need to move an object a short distance, carry it rather than slide or roll it if the weight is safe for you.</li>
<li>Practice the McGill “Big 3” (bird-dog, side plank, modified curl-up) a few times a week if you want a low-load way to rehearse stiffness. These were developed specifically to train the endurance and control the core uses in real life.</li>
<li>Stand and shift weight while doing ordinary tasks instead of locking into one posture.</li>
</ul>

<p>Start light. The goal is occasional purposeful stiffness, not exhaustion. Most people notice the midsection feels more “present” within a couple of weeks of simply carrying things more often.</p>

<h2>When to See a Doctor or Physiotherapist</h2>

<p>If ordinary carries reproduce sharp or radiating pain, or if you have the red-flag symptoms listed earlier, get assessed before loading the trunk further. A clinician familiar with spine biomechanics can distinguish simple under-use from issues that need specific treatment. For the common “my core feels switched off after desk days” pattern, gradual reintroduction of carries is usually the practical first step.</p>

<h2>FAQs</h2>

<p><strong>Is it normal for my midsection to feel weak even if I walk every day?</strong><br>
Yes. Unloaded walking is beneficial but does not create the same brief stiffness demand that carrying a load does. The two are complementary.</p>

<p><strong>Will carrying grocery bags actually train my core?</strong><br>
For most people, yes. The asymmetric or moderately heavy carry forces the trunk muscles to stiffen so the pelvis stays steady while you walk. That is the exact pattern the system evolved to handle.</p>

<p><strong>Should I stop doing sit-ups?</strong><br>
Repeated full sit-ups load the discs in flexion. Many spine researchers, including McGill, recommend limiting them in favor of exercises and real-life tasks that train stiffness without excessive bending. Occasional light flexion is not automatically harmful; high-volume sit-ups are the bigger concern.</p>

<p><strong>How heavy does a carry need to be?</strong><br>
It does not need to be maximal. A few kilograms held for a minute or two while walking is already more demand than empty-handed desk work. Progress gradually and stay pain-free.</p>

<p><strong>Can poor core control cause lower-back pain?</strong><br>
Reduced ability to create timely stiffness can make the spine more vulnerable when loads finally appear. It is one contributing factor among many (sleep, overall activity, prior injury, stress). Restoring ordinary carries often helps the everyday stiffness pattern without needing to treat the core as a separate “weak” part.</p>

<p><strong>Is this the same as needing more “core strength” for sports?</strong><br>
Related but not identical. Athletes often need higher levels of stiffness and endurance. Everyday people mainly need the occasional moderate demand that modern life removed. The underlying principle—proximal stiffness for safe force transfer—is the same.</p>

<h2>Conclusion</h2>

<p>Your core did not evolve for endless hours of unloaded sitting. It evolved to stiffen the trunk so you could carry useful things while moving. When that demand disappears, the cylinder around the spine simply becomes less practiced at creating the stiffness it is designed for. The fix is rarely dramatic. Carry ordinary objects a little more often, alternate sides, and give the system the brief, purposeful work it still expects. Most people find the midsection feels steadier within a short time, without ever needing a formal core routine.</p>

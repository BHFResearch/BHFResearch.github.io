You pull out of a lit parking lot and the first streetlamp wears a soft ring. Oncoming headlights throw short spikes. The porch light at home looks furry until you blink hard, and then it sharpens for a second. It is easy to wonder whether the eyes are failing, or whether night driving has quietly become unsafe.

A halo around a point of light at night is often ordinary optics, not a failing retina. A dry or uneven tear film, a wide pupil, uncorrected astigmatism, and early lens clouding all scatter light before it forms a crisp image. The ring is real light, misplaced. Most of these halos come and go with blinks, fatigue, and smudged lenses. A few patterns do not, and those are the ones worth treating as urgent.

## What Night Halos Look Like

People describe a pale ring, a glow, or a starburst around headlights, streetlamps, brake lights, and sometimes the moon. The ring may be white or faintly gray. It is locked to the light, not floating across the page the way a floater does. Blink, and it often shrinks. Look through a pinhole made with your fingers, and it often shrinks again, because you have cut off the messy edge of the optics.

Starbursts are the spiky cousin. They are common with a wide pupil, a small uncorrected astigmatism, or a dirty windshield acting as a second lens. Soft halos are more typical of forward scatter from the tear film or the lens of the eye. Colored rings, especially a rainbow sheen, are a different report. Those matter more if they arrive with pain, halos that will not blink away, or a visibly red eye.

Halos are not the dark spot you see after a camera flash, and they are not the threads that drift when you look at a bright sky. Afterimages sit where you were looking and fade in seconds. Floaters slide when the eye moves. A halo stays glued to the lamp.

## Why Point Lights Grow Rings After Dark

The eye is a good daytime camera and a compromised night camera. Fergus Campbell’s classic work on pupil size and image quality showed that a mid-size pupil gives the sharpest picture. At night the pupil opens to gather light. That wider opening lets in rays from the edge of the cornea and lens, where spherical aberration and small irregularities live. The brain gets a bright core plus a skirt of stray light. On a dark road that skirt reads as a halo.

Herschel Leibowitz and D. Alfred Owens measured a second night trick: night myopia. In dim light many people become a little more short-sighted, often on the order of half a diopter, sometimes more. Accommodation drifts, and the wide pupil exposes optical error that daylight had hidden. Distance lights go soft. If you already wear glasses that are slightly off, or if you are between prescriptions, night is when you notice.

The tear film is the third, and often the most fixable, layer. It is the first surface light hits. A thin spot, a mucus strand, or a contact lens that has dried at the edges diffracts light into a ring. Tom van den Berg’s straylight research treats this forward scatter as a measurable haze, separate from blur. You can have 20/20 letters on a bright chart and still complain of halos, because the chart does not imitate a headlight in the dark. Screen days make this worse. Incomplete blinks leave the tear film patchy by evening, so the drive home is when the rings show up.

## Common, Usually Benign Triggers

A long day on screens, dry indoor air, a fan on the face, or a windy walk can all roughen the tear film. Halos then ease after a few full blinks or a short rest with the eyes closed. Smudges, fingerprints, and a greasy windshield add a halo that disappears when you look beside the glass rather than through the smear.

Uncorrected astigmatism and an outdated distance prescription are everyday causes. So is the early need for a different pair for night driving. Multifocal glasses and some multifocal contact lenses split light on purpose. A faint ring around lamps can be the trade for a wider range of focus. It should be stable and explained, not sudden.

Contact lenses that are overworn, coated, or the wrong fit scatter light at the edge of the optic zone. Taking them out and comparing the same streetlamp often answers the question in a minute. Early cataract does the same job inside the eye. David Elliott’s work on disability glare showed that lens clouding can bother night driving before daytime blur feels dramatic. That is a reason to get checked, not a reason to panic on the first foggy evening.

Surgery leaves a temporary version. After laser vision correction or a lens implant, the optical zone can be smaller than the night pupil. Halos and starbursts are a known early trade-off for many people and usually settle as healing and neural adaptation proceed. A sudden increase months later is a reason to call the surgeon, not to wait it out alone.

## Less Common but Serious Causes

Rainbow halos with a brow ache, nausea, a red eye, and blurred vision can be corneal edema from a sudden rise in eye pressure, including acute angle-closure glaucoma. Harry Quigley’s work on angle closure makes the practical point: this is an eye emergency, not a glasses problem. The pupil may look mid-dilated and slow. The halo does not blink away. Same-day care matters because pressure can damage the optic nerve quickly.

A scratched or swollen cornea from overworn contacts, a foreign body, or keratitis can also throw rings, usually with pain, light sensitivity, and a feeling that something is in the eye. Infection under a contact lens is not a wait-until-Monday problem.

Halos are not the classic warning of a retinal tear. Flashes like lightning at the edge of vision, a shower of new floaters, or a curtain coming across the sight need an urgent retinal exam, even if no halo is present. A migraine aura can add zigzag lights or shimmer for twenty to forty minutes, often before a headache, and it moves. It should not be a stable ring around every streetlamp.

Some medicines widen the pupil or dry the eye, including certain antihistamines, antidepressants, and bladder drugs. A new halo week that starts with a new prescription is worth mentioning to the prescriber.

## Hidden Triggers in Ordinary Nights

Dirty glasses are the boring champion. Oil from the brow and a microfiber that has become a grease cloth will build a halo that no tear drop can fix. Headlight glare from newer LED lamps is also harsher than older bulbs. The eye did not get worse on Tuesday. The light source got smaller and bluer, and small bright sources advertise every optical flaw.

Low blood sugar, skipped meals, and heavy fatigue will not create a true halo by themselves, but they make you notice optical noise you usually ignore. Alcohol dries the tear film and slows pupil reactions. A late drive after wine is a bad test of whether the halos are “just you.”

If one eye sees the ring and the other does not, cover each eye in turn on a quiet street or in a doorway. A one-eye halo points to that eye’s surface, lens, or glasses lens, not to a general brain event.

## When to Worry

Get urgent care the same day if halos are colored or rainbow-like and come with eye pain, nausea, vomiting, a red eye, or sudden blur. That combination is the pattern clinicians do not want you to sleep on.

Call promptly, within a day or so, for halos that are new in one eye only, halos with light sensitivity and a foreign-body feeling, or rings that started after a contact lens overslept in the eye. A curtain, a burst of new floaters, or repeated flashes needs a same-day retinal check even without a halo.

A slow increase in night glare over months, especially after about fifty, is a reason for a routine eye exam rather than an emergency department visit. So is trouble reading signs until you are close, or feeling less sure on unfamiliar roads at night. Do not drive if the rings make lanes hard to judge. That is a practical stop, not a character test.

## Myths vs Facts

Myth: Halos mean you are going blind. Fact: Most night rings are scatter from tears, pupil size, or a small refractive miss. They need a cause, not a catastrophe story.

Myth: If the daytime eye chart is fine, the halos are imaginary. Fact: High-contrast letters in a bright room miss the straylight van den Berg measured. Night glare can be real in a person who still reads the 20/20 line.

Myth: Only cataracts cause halos. Fact: Dry eye, astigmatism, dirty lenses, and a wide pupil do it every evening in people with clear lenses.

Myth: Rainbow rings are always migraine. Fact: Migraine aura usually zigzags and moves, then leaves. A fixed rainbow around lights with pain and a red eye is a pressure problem until proved otherwise.

Myth: Eye drops you buy for redness will clear a halo. Fact: Whitening drops do not repair a tear film or a pressure spike, and some sting or rebound. The useful first step is a blink, clean glasses, and a proper exam if the halo stays.

## How to Manage Ordinary Night Halos

Blink fully several times before you judge a streetlamp. A partial blink leaves the center of the tear film thin. If your eyes feel sandy by evening, a plain lubricating drop used as directed can smooth the surface for the drive. Treat that as comfort, not as a diagnosis.

Clean both sides of your glasses, the windshield, and the contact-lens case you have been meaning to replace. Compare the same light with glasses on and off, and with one eye covered. Note whether the ring is in both eyes and whether it shrinks with a blink. That diary is more useful than a worried search at midnight.

Get a refraction that includes your night complaint, not only the bright-room chart. Mention astigmatism, night driving, and whether you are squinting to read signs. If you wear contacts, bring them in. Ask whether early lens changes are adding straylight if you are in the cataract age range and glare is slowly worsening.

On the road, look toward the right edge line when an oncoming car passes rather than into the lamps. Reduce dashboard brightness. Give yourself more following distance. Skip night driving on unfamiliar roads until an exam has sorted a new halo. Those are coping steps, not a substitute for checking a painful red eye.

## When to See a Doctor

See an optometrist or ophthalmologist if halos are new, one-sided, worsening over weeks, or paired with blur you cannot blink away. Mention rainbow color, pain, halos around every light rather than only the brightest ones, and any recent surgery or new medicine.

Use urgent eye care or emergency care for pain, nausea, a red eye, sudden vision loss, or a curtain over the sight. Tell them the word halo and how long it has lasted. A pressure check and a look at the cornea take minutes and change the plan completely if this is angle closure or a corneal infection.

Routine exams still matter if the only issue is gradual night glare. Elliott’s glare findings and van den Berg’s straylight work both say the same practical thing: daytime clarity does not retire the question.

## FAQs

### Are halos around streetlights normal?

Often, yes. A soft ring that shrinks when you blink, shows in both eyes, and tracks with dry air, fatigue, or a smudged lens is a common optical effect from the tear film and a wide night pupil. It is not automatically a disease. New, one-sided, or painful halos deserve an exam.

### Why do headlights look worse than daytime lights?

The pupil is larger, night myopia described by Leibowitz and Owens softens distance focus, and a small bright source on a black background advertises straylight. Daytime pupils are smaller, so edge optics stay hidden. LED headlights are also harsher point sources than older lamps.

### Can dry eyes really make a ring around a lamp?

Yes. The tear film is the first lens. A thin patch or mucus strand diffracts light into a halo or starburst. Screen use and incomplete blinks make this an evening problem. If drops and blinks do nothing, the scatter may be coming from the lens of the eye or from glasses instead.

### Do halos mean I have glaucoma?

Usually no. Chronic open-angle glaucoma is typically silent and does not announce itself with rings. Rainbow halos plus pain, nausea, and a red eye can be acute angle closure, which Quigley and others treat as an emergency. Color and pain are the clues, not the mere presence of a glow around a headlight.

### Should I stop driving at night?

Stop if you cannot confidently see lane edges, signs, or the gap to the next car. That is enough reason, even before a diagnosis. If halos are mild, both eyes, and long familiar, many people drive with clean lenses, more distance, and an updated prescription. A sudden change is a reason to pause night driving until someone examines the eyes.

### Can new glasses remove the halo?

They can, when the cause is defocus or astigmatism. They cannot fix a swollen cornea or advanced cataract scatter. A proper night-driving complaint, with glare included in the history, is the right request. Some people do better with a single-vision distance pair for driving than with a multifocal they use at the desk.

## Conclusion

A ring around a streetlamp is usually the eye admitting that night is a harder optical job than noon. A wide pupil, a patchy tear film, a small prescription miss, and early lens haze all paint light outside the point where it belongs. Blinks, clean lenses, and a refraction aimed at night driving solve a large share of them.

The pattern that should not wait is the rainbow halo with pain, nausea, or a red eye, and the halo that arrives with a curtain, a shower of floaters, or a contact lens that suddenly hurts. Those are different mechanisms from a tired tear film. If the rings are new and you are unsure which group you are in, an eye pressure check and a look at the cornea is a small visit for a large amount of reassurance.

You roll over and the arm does not come with you. It feels heavy, absent, or full of pins. For a second it does not seem attached. Then the blood-rush tingle starts, the hand prickles back to life, and within a minute or two you can make a fist again. That sequence is common. It is also the reason people sit up convinced something vascular or neurological just happened.

Most of the time it is neither. It is a nerve that spent part of the night under your head, folded at the elbow, or stretched overhead. Pressure and a bent joint slow conduction. When you move, the pressure comes off and the signal returns. The scare is real. The usual course is short.

## What This Numbness Feels Like

The classic version is a whole arm that feels dead on waking, often the one you were lying on. The skin is dull to touch. Moving the fingers feels delayed, as if the hand belongs to someone else. A hot-cold tingle follows, then normal strength. Some people only notice the hand, especially the thumb side or the ring and pinky side, depending on which nerve took the load.

A shorter cousin is the hand that tingles every morning from a flexed wrist, covered in more detail in [waking hand tingling](https://research.betterhealthfacts.com/articles/is-it-normal-for-your-hands-to-tingle-when-you-wake-up/). A dead arm is broader. The shoulder, forearm, and hand all feel offline, not just the fingers.

What is less ordinary is weakness that stays after the tingle fades. A wrist that will not lift, fingers that will not extend, or a grip that is still clumsy an hour later is no longer a simple sleep position. Numbness with a drooping face, slurred speech, or one-sided leg weakness on waking is an emergency, not a cramped nerve.

## Why Sleep Position Does This

Nerves are living cables. They need a small blood supply running in the sheath, and they do not like being pinched or held at a sharp angle for hours. Göran Lundborg’s work on nerve ischemia showed that conduction fails when pressure in the nerve exceeds the pressure in those tiny vessels, well before the axon itself is torn. A brief block is reversible. A long, hard block is not.

Herbert Seddon called the mild end of that spectrum neurapraxia: a conduction block, often at the myelin, with the axon still in continuity. Sydney Sunderland later graded nerve injuries by how much of the internal architecture was disrupted. A night of lying on the arm usually sits at the bottom of both scales. The signal stops. The cable is intact. Movement restores flow, and the pins-and-needles feeling is the nerve coming back online, the same pattern you get when a leg falls asleep, described in [legs that fall asleep](https://research.betterhealthfacts.com/articles/is-it-normal-for-your-legs-to-fall-asleep/).

Three positions account for most dead arms.

Lying on the arm, or draping it over the edge of a mattress, compresses the radial nerve where it wraps the humerus in the spiral groove. Textbooks still call the lasting version Saturday-night palsy, from an arm hung over a chair back. In sleep the same groove takes the weight of the head or the torso. The hand may feel numb on the back, and the wrist may be slow to lift.

Sleeping with the elbow deeply bent compresses the ulnar nerve in the cubital tunnel, behind the funny bone. Robert Feindel and Geoffrey Osborne described that tight spot. The pinky and ring finger go first. A related daytime version is covered in [pinky tingling when you lean on your elbow](https://research.betterhealthfacts.com/articles/is-it-normal-for-your-pinky-to-tingle-when-you-lean-on-your-elbow/).

An arm parked overhead, or a wrist curled hard under the pillow, loads the brachial plexus or the median nerve. Richard Gelberman measured how wrist flexion raises pressure in the carpal tunnel. George Phalen had already noticed that patients reproduced their night symptoms by holding that flexed pose. Overhead reaching adds stretch at the plexus, so the whole limb can feel absent rather than just the hand.

None of these require a diseased nerve. They require hours of stillness in a pose you would not hold while awake.

## Less Common but Serious Causes

A single episode that clears as you shake the arm is almost always positional. Patterns that repeat, spread, or leave weakness deserve a closer look.

Cervical nerve-root irritation can wake you with arm pain and a strip of numbness, often with neck stiffness. The tingle follows a dermatome, not the map of one peripheral nerve, and it may not care which side you slept on.

True radial-nerve palsy leaves a wrist drop that lasts hours or days. Spinner and colleagues mapped how compression at the spiral groove spares elbow extension but knocks out wrist and finger extension. That is no longer a two-minute recovery.

Diabetes, low thyroid function, alcohol-related neuropathy, and vitamin B12 deficiency make nerves easier to block and slower to recover. Pregnancy and recent weight gain do the same at the wrist and elbow, because fluid and soft tissue narrow the tunnels.

A stroke can announce itself on waking, but the usual clues are face, speech, or leg involvement, or weakness that does not march back with the tingle. Isolated arm numbness that returns while you are still sitting on the edge of the mattress is a poor match for that picture. If you are unsure, treat it as urgent.

## Hidden Triggers

A new pillow that lets the shoulder roll forward, or a soft mattress that lets the torso sink onto the arm, is a frequent change right before a run of dead arms. Stomach sleeping with both arms under the pillow folds the elbows and loads the shoulders at once.

Alcohol makes the same position more likely, because you move less and notice less. That is the original setting of Saturday-night palsy, even when the “chair” is now a couch.

Recent heavy gripping, a long day of elbow leaning, or a tight watch band can prime the nerve so a normal sleep position finishes the block. Cold bedrooms add a vascular squeeze on top of the compression, so the hand feels number and recovers more slowly.

## When to Worry

Shake the arm out and give it a few minutes of normal movement. Most positional episodes are gone, or nearly gone, by the time you are dressed.

Seek same-day care if the wrist still will not lift, the fingers will not spread, or a band of skin is still numb after an hour of being up and moving. Seek emergency care for face droop, speech change, confusion, chest pain, or a leg that is weak on the same side. A numb arm plus neck injury, fever, or rapidly worsening pain is not a sleep story.

Recurrent nightly whole-arm numbness, hand clumsiness that lingers into the afternoon, or visible thinning of the muscles at the base of the thumb or between the knuckles should be examined. Those are signs the compression is no longer limited to the night.

## Myths vs Facts

Myth: A dead arm on waking means a circulation problem in the heart. Fact: The usual event is local nerve ischemia from pressure. Heart symptoms are chest pressure, breathlessness, sweat, or pain into the jaw, not a limb that returns as soon as you roll off it.

Myth: If it tingles, the nerve is damaged. Fact: The tingle is usually recovery. Seddon’s neurapraxia resolves because the axon never snapped. Lasting weakness is the finding that changes the story.

Myth: You should vigorously fling the arm until it wakes. Fact: Gentle movement and a change of position are enough. Yanking a numb shoulder can strain the joint while the muscles are still offline.

Myth: Only older people get this. Fact: Anyone who sleeps on an arm, especially after alcohol or on a soft surface, can block a nerve for a few minutes. Age and diabetes make recovery slower, not exclusive.

## How to Manage It

Change the position before you try to interpret the symptom. Sit up, let the arm hang, and open and close the hand slowly. A short walk to the kitchen usually finishes the job. Do not test grip against a heavy object until the hand feels like yours again.

For the next night, keep the arm down by your side or resting on a pillow in front of the chest, elbow softer than a right angle, wrist near neutral. If you always roll onto one side, a body pillow in front gives the top arm somewhere to go that is not under your head. Stomach sleepers do better with a flatter pillow and arms down, even if that feels unfamiliar for a week.

Skip the tight wristband and the watch at night if the hand is the part that dies. If the pinky side is the part that dies, avoid extra elbow bend and daytime leaning on that elbow. If the back of the hand is the part that dies, do not sleep with that arm under the pillow or hanging off the mattress edge.

A hand that returns every morning but aches through the day can use the same neutral-wrist and softer-elbow habits while you arrange a check. Splints are sometimes useful at the wrist or elbow, but they should match the nerve involved, not be worn on a guess.

## When to See a Doctor

One cleared episode does not need a visit. Book one if the same arm goes numb most nights, if weakness outlasts the morning routine, or if neck pain and arm pain travel together. Mention diabetes, thyroid disease, pregnancy, or a recent injury, because those change how aggressive the workup should be.

A clinician may test which nerve is slow, look at the neck, and only sometimes order conduction studies. Susan Mackinnon’s compression research is a reminder that the site of pinch is often a tunnel, and the fix is often position and load, not a scan. Imaging is for cases that do not behave like a night of pressure.

## FAQs

### Is a numb arm on waking a sign of a stroke?

Usually no. Positional numbness returns as you move and stays in the arm you were lying on. Stroke warning signs include face droop, trouble speaking, confusion, or weakness in the leg as well as the arm. Those need emergency care even if they showed up on waking.

### Why does the arm tingle as it wakes up?

Pressure had slowed blood flow inside the nerve and blocked conduction. Lundborg showed that this ischemia reverses when pressure drops. The prickling is fibers restarting, not a new injury, as long as strength returns with it.

### Can sleeping with my arm overhead cause this?

Yes. Overhead reach stretches the brachial plexus and can numb the whole limb. A deeply bent elbow hits the ulnar nerve. A curled wrist hits the median nerve. All three are ordinary sleep postures.

### How long should a dead arm take to recover?

Most clear within a few minutes of sitting up and moving. If wrist lift, finger spread, or a patch of skin is still clearly abnormal after an hour of normal use, get it checked the same day.

### Does alcohol make a dead arm more likely?

Yes. You shift less, and you may drape the arm over a couch edge or under your head for longer. That is the setting behind the old name Saturday-night palsy, a radial-nerve block at the spiral groove.

### Should I wear a brace at night?

Only if a clinician has identified the nerve and the posture. A neutral wrist support helps some median-nerve nights. An elbow pad that limits deep bend helps some ulnar-nerve nights. A random brace on the wrong joint does not.

## Conclusion

Waking with a dead arm is usually a nerve that spent the night under pressure or around a sharp bend. The axon is intact, the block is brief, and the tingle is the signal returning. Seddon, Sunderland, and Lundborg described that mild end of nerve injury long before soft mattresses and overhead sleeping made it a weekly event for some people. Shake it out, change how the arm rests, and treat lasting weakness or any face and speech change as a different problem.

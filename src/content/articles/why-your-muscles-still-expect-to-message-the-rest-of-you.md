<p>For most of medical history, muscle was furniture that moved bones. It burned fuel, it tore and repaired, and that was the job description. Then researchers started drawing blood during a bicycle ride and found a cytokine rising from the working legs themselves — not from an infection, not from fat, from the fibers that were shortening and releasing.</p>

<p>Bente Klarlund Pedersen and her group in Copenhagen spent the 1990s and 2000s making that observation unavoidable. Interleukin-6, long filed as an inflammatory alarm, poured out of contracting muscle in amounts that tracked intensity and duration. Mark Febbraio and Adam Steensberg showed the same molecule leaving the femoral vein of an exercising leg in net surplus. The tissue that looks like a motor is also a gland. The messages it sends are now called myokines.</p>

<p>That matters because the rest of you still expects the mail. Liver, adipose tissue, bone, endothelium, brain and circulating immune cells all have receptors for products of contraction. A body that sits from breakfast to dinner is not only under-loaded mechanically. It is under-briefed chemically. The mismatch is quiet. Nothing hurts in the first week. The conversation simply never starts.</p>

<h2>What This System Was Built to Do</h2>

<p>A myokine is a peptide or small protein released by skeletal muscle, especially during and after contraction, that acts on the muscle itself or on distant organs. Pedersen’s laboratory framed the idea after they could not explain exercise IL-6 as a leak from damaged fibers. The rise was too clean, too reproducible, and it happened in healthy people who were not injured.</p>

<p>IL-6 is the prototype and the most misunderstood. In infection it is part of a fever-and-CRP cascade. After a long walk or a hard interval it behaves differently. Muscle-derived IL-6 helps mobilize fuel, increases hepatic glucose output when the working tissue needs it, and — in that context — is followed by a rise in anti-inflammatory mediators such as IL-10 and IL-1 receptor antagonist. The same name, a different brief. Context is the whole story.</p>

<p>The catalogue grew. Irisin, cleaved from FNDC5 in muscle under PGC-1α drive, was described by Pontus Boström and Bruce Spiegelman as a signal that can nudge white fat toward a more beige, heat-capable phenotype. BDNF is made in muscle as well as brain; contraction raises it, and the circulating and local pools sit in the same family of “use it and it talks” chemistry that we described in <a href="https://research.betterhealthfacts.com/articles/why-your-bdnf-still-expects-movement-and-novelty/">why your BDNF still expects movement and novelty</a>. Other contraction products include IL-15, which talks to fat and muscle protein balance; meteorin-like; SPARC; and a growing list of peptides that have not yet earned household names.</p>

<p>None of this required a gym in the environment that built it. Hunting, carrying, climbing, walking between water and shade, and the small emergencies of a day on foot were enough contraction to keep the mail moving. The system does not demand a personal record. It demands that large muscle groups shorten against resistance often enough that the endocrine side of the fiber stays employed.</p>

<h2>The Mismatch: Motors That Rarely Write</h2>

<p>Modern sitting does two things at once. It removes the mechanical strain bones and tendons expect, and it silences the chemical briefing other organs expect. You can eat well and still leave liver, fat and immune cells without the myokine pulse that used to arrive every few hours.</p>

<p>The insulin side of this is already familiar. Contracting muscle pulls glucose in through GLUT4 by a route that does not wait for a large insulin surge. That is one reason daytime walking after meals still pays, as we laid out in <a href="https://research.betterhealthfacts.com/articles/why-your-insulin-still-expects-daytime-muscle/">why your insulin still expects daytime muscle</a>. Myokines sit next to that story rather than inside it. They are how the working tissue announces the event to neighbors that are not themselves shortening.</p>

<p>Immune tone is the piece people feel last and miss first. Regular contraction is associated with a lower resting inflammatory set point in observational work and in training studies. The mechanism is not “exercise melts stress.” It is closer to a scheduled anti-inflammatory letter after a scheduled inflammatory-looking spike. Skip the contraction and you skip both the spike and the letter. What remains is the low-grade background of sitting, visceral fat and sleep loss — a different IL-6 neighborhood entirely.</p>

<p>Brain effects are slower to name and easier to notice. People who walk daily often describe clearer thinking than the calorie math can explain. Some of that is blood flow and temperature. Some of it is the overlap between muscle signals, BDNF, and the fact that a walking human is also sending vestibular, visual and hippocampal updates. Muscle mail is one page of a thicker envelope.</p>

<p>Mitochondria are both cause and consequence. Fibers that never work do not keep as many of the organelles that make the myokine response robust, which is part of the quieter capacity described in <a href="https://research.betterhealthfacts.com/articles/why-your-mitochondria-still-expect-movement-and-night/">why your mitochondria still expect movement and night</a>. The gland and the engine live in the same cell. Neglect one and you dull the other.</p>

<h2>What the Research Actually Shows</h2>

<p>Steensberg, van Hall, Febbraio and Pedersen measured net IL-6 release across the exercising human leg and showed it could account for the systemic rise. Blocking the molecule in animal and human models blunts some of the metabolic effects of the bout. That does not make IL-6 a miracle. It makes it a participant.</p>

<p>Training studies consistently show that a program of walking, cycling or resistance work lowers resting CRP and several inflammatory markers in people who start with a high baseline. The benefit is larger when the starting point is worse, which is the opposite of a vanity effect. Michael Gleeson and others mapped how moderate regular work supports immune surveillance while extreme unrelieved load can temporarily suppress it. Dose still matters. The ancestral pattern was frequent moderate contraction with occasional hard days, not a sedentary week capped by one annihilating session.</p>

<p>Irisin’s human story is messier than the first mouse papers. Assays disagreed. Effects at physiologic circulating levels are still debated. What is not debated is that FNDC5 expression in muscle responds to contraction and to the PGC-1α program that endurance work turns on. Even if irisin is not the single courier some headlines wanted, the principle stands: working muscle changes the secretome.</p>

<p>Resistance work has its own envelope. Mechanical tension, metabolic stress and micro-repair recruit a slightly different mix than a long walk. Both count. The unused option is the all-day quiet that produces almost no letter at all.</p>

<h2>Hidden Triggers That Keep the Mail Unsent</h2>

<p>The obvious one is the chair. The less obvious ones are “I already exercised, so the other fourteen hours can be still,” very short isolated gym moves that never recruit the big locomotors, and days so fragmented that you never accumulate ten continuous minutes of walking. Myokine release scales with the mass of muscle working and the time it works. Calf raises at a standing desk help veins. They are not a substitute for a walk that uses hips and trunk.</p>

<p>Another trigger is treating muscle as only a calorie sink. If the goal is a number on a wearable, people often choose the shortest hardest protocol and then sit. Hard protocols have a place. They still leave most of the day without the low-grade chemical mail a foraging day would have sent in the background.</p>

<p>Illness, short sleep and large late meals change the baseline the myokines land on. The same walk does not write the same letter on three hours of sleep. That is not a reason to skip the walk. It is a reason not to expect one bout to cancel a week of mismatch.</p>

<h2>Myths vs Facts</h2>

<p><strong>Myth:</strong> Muscle is only for strength and shape.<br />
<strong>Fact:</strong> Contracting muscle is one of the largest endocrine tissues you own. Its products brief liver, fat, bone, vessels and brain.</p>

<p><strong>Myth:</strong> Any IL-6 rise is bad inflammation.<br />
<strong>Fact:</strong> Muscle-derived IL-6 after work is a different biological sentence from IL-6 during infection or from visceral fat at rest.</p>

<p><strong>Myth:</strong> Only long endurance sessions count.<br />
<strong>Fact:</strong> Both prolonged locomotion and resistance work change the secretome. What barely counts is all-day stillness.</p>

<p><strong>Myth:</strong> A supplement can replace the walk.<br />
<strong>Fact:</strong> No capsule reproduces the mix, the timing, or the local-to-systemic pattern of a contracting limb.</p>

<p><strong>Myth:</strong> If you are not sore, nothing happened.<br />
<strong>Fact:</strong> Myokine release does not require delayed soreness. Soreness is a repair signal, not the postage stamp.</p>

<h2>How to Give the System What It Still Expects</h2>

<p>Walk after meals when you can. Large locomotor muscles working for ten to twenty minutes send both a glucose and a myokine message. You do not need a sport watch to start.</p>

<p>Stand and carry. A bag of groceries, a child, a water jug — load that uses trunk and hips — is closer to the original brief than an isolated machine that leaves the rest of you quiet.</p>

<p>Lift something two or three days a week. Mechanical tension is a different letter from a walk. The combination is what a body on varied terrain used to get without naming it.</p>

<p>Protect sleep and an overnight fast so the next day’s contraction lands on a tissue that can answer. Muscle clocks still expect daytime load. Night is for a different shift.</p>

<p>Do not wait for motivation to feel hormonal. The first ten minutes of walking are how the gland clocks in. The feeling often arrives after the chemistry has already started.</p>

<h2>When to See a Doctor</h2>

<p>New unexplained muscle weakness, pain at rest, dark urine after exercise, or a sudden drop in walking capacity is not a myokine problem to self-coach. Those are medical problems. People with heart, lung or metabolic disease still benefit from movement, but the dose should be set with a clinician who knows their limits. This article is about a missing everyday signal, not a treatment plan for myopathy, heart failure or uncontrolled diabetes.</p>

<h2>FAQs</h2>

<h3>Are myokines the same as hormones?</h3>
<p>They behave like hormones when they travel in blood and act on distant organs. Many also act locally on the same muscle or nearby fat. The practical point is the same: contraction writes messages sitting does not.</p>

<h3>Does stretching count?</h3>
<p>Gentle stretching is useful for comfort and range. It is a weak myokine stimulus compared with walking or lifting because little muscle mass is working against much load.</p>

<h3>Is high-intensity interval training enough by itself?</h3>
<p>Intervals can produce a large pulse. They do not recreate the frequent low-grade mail of a day that also includes walking and carrying. Use both if you can. Do not trade a whole day’s locomotion for twelve brutal minutes and a chair.</p>

<h3>Can older adults still send these signals?</h3>
<p>Yes. Aging muscle releases myokines, and training studies in older people still change inflammatory and metabolic markers. The dose may be smaller and the recovery longer. The need for the letter does not retire.</p>

<h3>Why do I feel better the day after I walk even if I did not lose weight?</h3>
<p>Weight is a slow ledger. Myokines, blood flow, sleep pressure and mood chemistry move faster. Feeling better the next day is often the briefing arriving, not fat leaving.</p>

<h3>Do I need to measure anything?</h3>
<p>No clinical myokine panel is part of ordinary care. The useful measurements are still the ones you can feel and schedule: minutes of walking, days you lifted, nights you slept.</p>

<h2>Conclusion</h2>

<p>Your muscles were never only engines. They were also a broadcasting system for a body that moved to live. Pedersen, Febbraio, Spiegelman and the labs that followed did not invent that job. They named the mail that walking had been sending without a vocabulary.</p>

<p>You do not have to become an athlete to reopen the channel. You have to stop treating stillness as the default and contraction as a hobby. A walk after lunch, a carry on the way home, a few loaded efforts each week — that is the brief the rest of you is still waiting to read.</p>

You open your eyes and there it is again—that small, slightly sticky or crumbly bit of material in the inner corner, sometimes enough to make your lashes feel glued for a second. Most people wipe it away without thinking. The medical name is rheum (sometimes called gound), though almost no one uses it in conversation. You are more likely to hear “sleep crust,” “eye sand,” or “eye boogers.” For the large majority of people it is simply the overnight version of the eye’s ordinary cleaning system.

## What This Symptom Feels Like

The material is usually pale yellow, cream-colored, or translucent. It can be dry and flaky or soft and sticky. It gathers most often in the inner canthus (the corner nearest the nose) and along the lash line. A light amount that clears with a gentle wipe or a few blinks is the everyday version. Some mornings there is more, especially if the air was dry, you slept with a fan running, or allergy season is active. The eyes themselves may feel a little gritty until the first blinks restore the tear film—something explored in more detail in our piece on <a href="https://research.betterhealthfacts.com/articles/is-it-normal-for-your-eyes-to-feel-gritty-in-the-morning/">morning gritty eyes</a>.

## Common Causes

During the day the eyelids act like windshield wipers. Each blink spreads a three-layer tear film (mucus from the conjunctiva, watery tears, and oil from the meibomian glands) and sweeps debris toward the tear ducts that drain into the nose. Once the eyes close for sleep, blinking stops for hours. Production of the watery part of the tear film also slows. Mucus, oil, shed epithelial cells, and any dust that was already present simply sit in place. Gravity tends to pool the mixture in the corners. Overnight evaporation concentrates what remains, so by morning it has dried into the familiar crust.

Heinrich Meibom described the oil-producing glands in the eyelids in the seventeenth century; those glands still supply the oily outer layer of the tear film that slows evaporation. When the film stagnates, a bit of that oil ends up in the morning residue as well. Ophthalmologists such as Jeff Pettey note that nearly everyone produces some rheum; the amount simply varies.

## Why This Happens

The process is mechanical and continuous. The conjunctiva and meibomian glands keep secreting while you sleep. Without the mechanical flushing of blinks, the secretions and debris accumulate. The closed-eye environment is slightly warmer and more humid than open air, yet the watery component still slowly evaporates, leaving a more concentrated, stickier mix. This is why the crust is almost always worst on waking and why it clears quickly once blinking resumes. People who sleep with incomplete eyelid closure (lagophthalmos) or who have a thinner tear film often notice more residue because evaporation is faster.

## Less Common but Serious Causes

Extra crust, especially if it is yellow-green, accompanied by redness, pain, light sensitivity, or vision change, can signal bacterial conjunctivitis or other infection. Blepharitis (inflammation of the eyelid margins) and meibomian gland dysfunction commonly produce more morning debris because oil flow is blocked or the lid margins stay inflamed. Allergic conjunctivitis can increase mucus production overnight. A blocked tear duct may also allow more material to remain. These situations are less common than ordinary rheum but are the main reasons clinicians ask about color, volume, and associated symptoms.

## Hidden Triggers

Dry indoor air, overnight fans or air conditioning, seasonal allergies, incomplete blinks during screen-heavy days (which can leave the surface drier by bedtime), and certain medications that reduce tear production all raise the chance of noticeable morning crust. Contact-lens wearers sometimes see more residue if the lenses or lens-care solutions irritate the surface. Sleeping on one side can make that eye’s crust slightly more obvious simply because of gravity and contact with the pillow.

## When to Worry

See an eye-care professional if the discharge is clearly yellow or green, if the lids are stuck shut and hard to open, if one eye is much worse than the other, or if you have pain, marked redness, swelling, or any change in vision. Persistent crusting that continues well into the day, or that returns despite simple hygiene, also deserves a look—especially if you already know you have dry eye or blepharitis. Ordinary pale, modest morning residue that clears with blinking or a gentle wipe is not a reason for concern.

## Myths vs Facts

**Myth:** Morning eye crust means your eyes are dirty or infected.  
**Fact:** Pale or cream-colored residue is the normal overnight accumulation of mucus, oil, cells, and debris. Color and accompanying symptoms matter more than the simple presence of crust.

**Myth:** You should never touch it.  
**Fact:** Gentle removal with a clean, moist cloth or tissue is fine. Aggressive rubbing is what irritates the surface.

**Myth:** Only children get “eye boogers.”  
**Fact:** Adults produce the same material; children may simply notice or mention it more often.

## How to Manage It

A warm (not hot) compress for a few minutes on waking softens any dried material so it wipes away easily. Clean the lid margins gently with a diluted baby shampoo or a commercial lid wipe if crust is frequent. Avoid sleeping with a strong fan or air conditioner aimed at your face. A bedside humidifier can help in dry seasons. During the day, full blinks and occasional screen breaks keep the tear film healthier; the role of complete blinks is covered in our articles on <a href="https://research.betterhealthfacts.com/articles/why-your-meibomian-glands-still-expect-a-full-blink/">meibomian glands</a> and <a href="https://research.betterhealthfacts.com/articles/why-your-tear-film-still-expects-a-full-blink/">tear-film stability</a>. If allergies are active, treating them often reduces overnight mucus. Most people need nothing more than these simple steps.

## When to See a Doctor

Book an appointment if crust is heavy, colored, or accompanied by pain, redness, light sensitivity, or vision changes, or if the lids remain stuck after gentle cleaning. People with known blepharitis, rosacea, or chronic dry eye may need targeted treatment (warm compresses, lid hygiene, or prescribed drops) rather than home measures alone. An eye-care professional can distinguish ordinary rheum from infection or gland dysfunction quickly.

## FAQs

**Is a little bit of white or cream-colored crust every morning normal?**  
Yes. It is the expected result of mucus, oil, cells, and debris collecting while blinking is paused.

**Why is there sometimes more crust in one eye?**  
Sleep position, slight differences in tear-film quality, or a mild one-sided allergy or irritation can make one side more noticeable. Persistent one-sided heavy discharge should be checked.

**Can dry air or a fan make it worse?**  
Yes. Faster evaporation overnight concentrates the remaining material and can leave more visible crust or a grittier sensation on waking.

**Does more crust mean I have an infection?**  
Not by itself. Pale, modest amounts are normal. Yellow-green color, pain, redness, or difficulty opening the eyes raise the possibility of infection or significant inflammation and warrant an exam.

**Will lid hygiene or warm compresses help?**  
For most people they reduce the amount of residue and make removal easier. They are also first-line care for mild blepharitis.

**Should I worry if my eyes feel sticky but the crust is clear?**  
Clear or stringy mucus is more often linked to dry eye or allergy than to infection. If it is bothersome or increasing, an eye exam can clarify the cause.

## Conclusion

Waking up with a small amount of crusty material in the corners of the eyes is one of the most ordinary consequences of sleeping with your eyes closed. The same secretions that keep the surface healthy during the day simply pause their normal clearing process overnight and dry in place. Color, volume, and any extra symptoms are the practical guides: pale and modest is expected; colored, heavy, or painful is worth a professional look. A warm compress, gentle cleaning, and attention to bedroom air usually keep the everyday version from becoming an annoyance.

You close your eyes for a minute and the dark is not dark. A gray field sits there, then a wash of dull red, a drifting lattice, a soft spiral, or a spot that blooms and fades. It is more obvious at night, after a long screen, or in the minute before sleep. It can feel private and slightly wrong, as if the eyes should switch off the way a lamp does.

They do not. Closed lids only dim the world. The retina and the visual cortex keep a low murmur going, and that murmur has a shape. For most people the shapes are ordinary physiology: leftover light through the lids, spontaneous firing in the dark, and the brain’s habit of organizing noise into edges. The patterns are worth knowing, because a few cousins of the same sensation are not ordinary at all.

## What Closed-Eye Patterns Feel Like

The everyday version is bilateral and soft. Both eyes, or the whole field, rather than a hard spark in one corner. People describe brain-gray instead of black, a deep red or purple cast, slow clouds, checkerboards, tunnels, cobwebs, or tiny moving points. The image changes if you press the lids, yawn, or look toward a bright window and then shut the eyes. It usually fades when you open them onto a lit room. It does not come with pain, a curtain across the side vision, or a shower of new specks that stay when the eyes are open.

A related and still common pattern is the brief spark after you rub. Those are pressure phosphenes, the same family as the [stars you see when you rub your eyes](https://research.betterhealthfacts.com/articles/is-it-normal-to-see-stars-when-you-rub-your-eyes/). Another is the colored blob that hangs for a few seconds after you look away from a phone or a window. That is an afterimage, not a new growth in the eye. A third is the geometric drift that arrives as you are falling asleep, clearer if the room is quiet and the day was visually busy.

What is less ordinary: a new flash at the edge of vision with the eyes open, especially if it repeats in one eye, a sudden crowd of floaters, a shadow like a curtain, zigzag lines that expand over minutes with or without a headache, or formed scenes — faces, text, animals — that persist while you are fully awake and looking at the room.

## Why the Dark Is Never Blank

In the 1860s Gustav Fechner, working on psychophysics, named the faint light of the closed or darkened eye Eigenlicht, one’s own light. Later writers called the gray field eigengrau, intrinsic gray. The point was simple. Absolute black is an idea. A living retina does not output zero.

Rod cells, built for dim light, fire a little even in darkness. Horace Barlow’s work on retinal noise showed why that matters: the eye’s absolute threshold is limited by its own dark noise, not only by how few photons arrive. Spontaneous events in the photoreceptors and the retinal circuitry become a faint grain. Visual cortex does not leave grain alone. It prefers contours, so the grain is often read as lattices, waves, or tunnels.

Heinrich Klüver, describing the geometry of visual hallucinations in the 1920s and 1940s, called these repeating shapes form constants: gratings, cobwebs, spirals, and tunnels. You do not need a drug to meet a mild version. Pressure on the globe, the hypnagogic slide into sleep, or a long stare at a bright pattern can recruit the same family of forms. The wiring prefers them.

Lids are also poor blackout curtains. They pass longer, redder wavelengths more easily than blue, which is why the field often looks maroon or dull orange in a lit room and grayer in a dark one. Close your eyes toward a window and the red deepens. Turn away and it cools. That shift is light, not imagination.

## Common Reasons the Patterns Show Up

Screen afterimages are the modern trigger. A high-contrast page, a game, or a sunset leaves photoreceptors temporarily bleached. Complementary colors bloom when you shut the eyes. Hermann von Helmholtz mapped these negative afterimages in the nineteenth century. They are stronger if you have been blinking less, which is the usual screen habit, and they clear within seconds to a couple of minutes.

Pressure and posture add another layer. A hand on the face, a tight sleep mask, rubbing, or even a hard yawn can deform the globe enough to stimulate retinal cells. Jan Evangelista Purkinje described these mechanical phosphenes in 1819. They are harmless in passing and a bad habit if rubbing becomes the way you “check” them. Rubbing also worsens the [floaters](https://research.betterhealthfacts.com/articles/is-it-normal-to-see-floaters-in-your-vision/) some people already notice.

Sleep onset is a third ordinary setting. Alfred Maury wrote about hypnagogic images in the 1840s, the pictures that arrive in the border between wake and sleep. Daniel Schacter’s later review found them widely reported. Geometric patterns, faces that will not hold still, or a sense that the dark is moving are common in that window. They tend to vanish once sleep actually starts, or once you sit up and turn a light on.

Low ambient light makes the same noise easier to notice. Indoor rooms are dim next to daylight, which is part of why the [pupils still expect outdoor light](https://research.betterhealthfacts.com/articles/why-your-pupils-still-expect-outdoor-light/). In a dim evening the contrast between retinal chatter and the outside world shrinks, so the chatter wins.

## Less Common but Serious Lookalikes

A retinal tear or detachment can announce itself with flashes, new floaters, or a curtain. Those flashes are usually seen with the eyes open, often at the side, and they may come with a sudden increase in specks or a shadow. Charles Schepens and later Norman Byer described how vitreous traction on the retina produces this photopsia. Closed-eye color clouds that leave when you open your eyes are a different story. New one-sided flashes that stay in open-eye vision are not.

Migraine aura can paint zigzag fortification lines, shimmering heat-haze, or a growing blind spot, usually over five to sixty minutes. Karl Lashley mapped his own aura in 1941. Aristides Leão’s spreading depression is the cortical wave often linked to it. Aura can occur with little or no headache. It is typically binocular, builds and marches, and then resolves. A first aura, an aura that lasts hours, or aura with weakness or trouble speaking needs a clinician, not a self-label.

Charles Bonnet syndrome is another relative, not the everyday closed-eye pattern. Charles Bonnet described formed visual hallucinations in the eighteenth century in the setting of vision loss. People with significant eye disease may see detailed scenes they know are not real. Insight is often preserved. That is a reason to have eyes examined if complex figures appear while you are awake and looking around, especially if sight has already been fading.

Rarely, occipital seizures or vascular events produce persistent colored patterns. They come with other signs: confusion, language change, a field cut that does not clear, or a sudden severe headache. Pattern alone, soft and familiar, behind closed lids, is not that picture.

## Hidden Triggers

A bright phone in a dark room is the most reliable way to manufacture an afterimage. The pupil is wide, the screen is the only light, and the bleach is uneven. Dry eyes and incomplete blinks make you stare longer, so the afterimage lasts longer.

Sleep debt and the hypnagogic state amplify geometry. So does a long day of high-contrast work, stripes, spreadsheets, or highway lanes. Pressure from a sleep mask, a hand under the cheek, or vigorous rubbing recruits Purkinje phosphenes on top of the noise.

Some medicines and migraine-prone brains lower the threshold for visual shimmer. Alcohol, sudden standing with a gray-out, and hard coughing can add brief sparkles of a circulatory kind. Those are seconds long and tied to the maneuver. A new medicine that coincides with persistent open-eye patterns is worth mentioning to the prescriber.

## When to Worry

Seek urgent eye care the same day for a new shower of floaters, repeated flashes in one eye with the eyes open, a curtain or shadow in the side vision, or a sudden drop in sight. Those are retinal-tear rules, not closed-eye curiosity.

Seek urgent medical care for patterns that arrive with a sudden severe headache, weakness, slurred speech, double vision that does not clear, fainting, or confusion. A first-ever marching zigzag, an aura lasting more than an hour, or formed hallucinations while awake and eyes-open also deserve a proper look, even if they turn out to be migraine or Charles Bonnet.

You can watch and adjust habits if the shapes are soft, both eyes, mostly behind closed lids or in the dark, gone when you look at a normally lit room, and familiar over months without a change in vision.

## Myths vs Facts

Myth: Closed-eye patterns mean the retina is detaching. Fact: Detachment warnings are usually open-eye flashes, new floaters, and a curtain. Soft bilateral clouds behind the lids are retinal and cortical noise.

Myth: You should see pure black if the eyes are healthy. Fact: Fechner’s Eigenlicht is the normal floor. Black is what the lid looks like from outside, not what the retina reports.

Myth: Colors behind the lids are always a migraine. Fact: Migraine aura has a march, a time course, and often a zigzag edge. A drifting maroon field after a screen does not.

Myth: Rubbing the eyes is a useful test. Fact: Rubbing creates phosphenes and can scratch the cornea. It does not diagnose anything.

Myth: Noticing them more with age means vision is failing. Fact: Floaters and a slower pupil can make entoptic effects easier to see. A real change in acuity, field, or new flashes still needs an exam, not a guess.

## How to Settle the Field

Give the eyes a lit, not glaring, room before you close them. A lamp off to the side beats a phone six inches from the face in the dark. Blink fully a few times so the afterimage is not fed by a long stare.

Skip the rub. If the lids feel gritty, use a plain lubricating drop that your clinician has said is fine for you, and rest the gaze on something distant for twenty seconds. Patterns often quiet once the recent bleach fades.

If geometry surges as you fall asleep, treat it as a border-state event. A regular wind-down, a dimmer last hour, and less high-contrast scrolling reduce how loud it feels. You do not need to fear it into staying.

People who get migraine aura can keep a simple note of timing, one eye or both, and whether a headache follows. That note is more useful than another search at midnight.

## When to See a Doctor

Book a routine eye exam if you have never had one as an adult, if glasses are old, or if closed-eye patterns have started alongside worse night driving, more floaters than your usual, or a change in how straight lines look. An optometrist or ophthalmologist can separate entoptic noise from vitreous traction.

Go sooner, same day if you can, for one-sided open-eye flashes, a new floater storm, or a shadow. Go to emergency care if the visual change comes with neurological signs. Mention medicines, recent migraine, and whether the images are geometric noise or formed scenes. The distinction changes the workup.

## FAQs

### Why do I see gray or red instead of black?

Lids transmit some light, especially red wavelengths, and photoreceptors fire a little even in the dark. Fechner called that residual field Eigenlicht. A gray or maroon wash with the eyes closed is the usual result, not a stain on the eye.

### Are the grids and tunnels a hallucination?

They are a mild, expected organization of visual noise. Klüver’s form constants — lattices, spirals, tunnels — show up in pressure phosphenes and at sleep onset as well as in stronger hallucinations. Context matters. Soft shapes only behind closed lids are not the same as persistent formed scenes while you are awake.

### Can a phone cause this?

Yes. A bright screen in a dim room bleaches cones unevenly and leaves a complementary afterimage, the phenomenon Helmholtz described. It should fade within minutes of looking at ordinary room light. If open-eye flashes continue after that, treat them as a separate symptom.

### Does this mean I am getting a retinal detachment?

Not by itself. Detachment and retinal tear tend to produce open-eye flashes, a sudden increase in floaters, and sometimes a curtain, as described in the vitreous-traction literature from Schepens and Byer. Bilateral closed-eye color without those signs is a different mechanism.

### Why is it worse at night or when I am falling asleep?

Darkness removes the outside image that normally masks retinal noise, and the hypnagogic state, reviewed by Schacter and described since Maury, lets spontaneous visual activity surface. Sitting up and turning on a moderate light usually dissolves it.

### When should I stop assuming it is normal?

If the pattern is new and one-sided with the eyes open, if floaters suddenly multiply, if a shadow appears, if zigzags march for the first time, or if you see detailed figures while awake and looking at the room. Those need an exam. A familiar closed-eye cloud that clears in the light does not.

## Conclusion

The eye is not a camera with a shutter that goes black. It is a noisy sensor under a thin lid, wired to a cortex that would rather draw an edge than leave a field empty. Gray, dull red, a drifting grid, a sleep-onset spiral: that is the ordinary report of a system still on. Learn the difference between that murmur and a new flash, a curtain, or a marching zigzag, and the closed-eye show can go back to being a curiosity instead of a worry.

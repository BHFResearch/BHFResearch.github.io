You finish a two-hour video call, close the laptop, and a dull ache starts behind your eyes or across your forehead. Your neck feels tight, the room seems a little too bright, and you just want to look at something far away. This pattern is extremely common with back-to-back video meetings and long remote-work sessions.

## What This Headache Usually Feels Like

Most people describe a pressure or band-like tightness across the forehead, behind the eyes, or at the temples. It often builds gradually rather than striking suddenly. The pain is usually mild to moderate, improves once you step away from the screen, and may come with dry, gritty, or tired eyes, slight neck stiffness, and a feeling of mental fog.

It is different from a sudden, severe headache that wakes you from sleep or arrives with vomiting, confusion, or one-sided weakness. Those need prompt medical attention.

## Common Reasons a Video Call Triggers a Headache

Several ordinary factors stack up during a long call:

- **Reduced blinking and eye strain.** When you stare at a screen, your blink rate drops. James Sheedy and other researchers who studied computer vision syndrome noted that fewer blinks leave the tear film unstable, so the eye surface dries and the focusing muscles work harder.
- **Fixed near focus.** Your ciliary muscles stay contracted to keep the screen sharp. After an hour or two the effort itself can produce an ache behind the eyes.
- **Forward-head posture.** Most people lean slightly toward the camera or screen. This loads the upper neck and suboccipital muscles, which refer pain into the head.
- **Lighting and glare.** A bright window behind you, a dark room with a glowing screen, or reflections on glasses all increase visual effort.
- **Dehydration and caffeine shifts.** Long calls often mean you forget to drink water or delay your usual coffee, both of which can contribute to a headache.
- **Jaw tension.** Focusing on looking “engaged” can lead to subtle clenching that feeds into temple and forehead pain.

These are the same mechanisms that appear in longer screen days; a video call simply concentrates them because you are both watching and being watched.

## Why the Body Responds This Way

Your visual system evolved for scanning distant landscapes and near objects at arm’s length, with frequent changes in focus and plenty of natural blinks. A video call asks for sustained near focus, minimal head movement, and reduced blinking—all at once. The extra muscular work and the drying of the ocular surface send signals that the brain registers as discomfort or pain.

At the same time the neck is held in a relatively fixed position. Small postural muscles that are meant for brief glances and balance stay contracted. Over time that sustained load produces the familiar “band” across the forehead or the ache at the base of the skull.

Researchers who study digital eye strain (including work building on Sheedy’s early descriptions and later studies on blink rate by Kazuo Tsubota) consistently show that incomplete blinks and reduced blink frequency are central to the problem. The headache is usually the downstream result of that visual and postural load rather than a primary headache disorder.

## Less Common but More Serious Possibilities

Most post-call headaches are benign. Still, a few situations deserve attention:

- A sudden, severe “thunderclap” headache
- Headache with neurological symptoms (weakness, speech trouble, vision loss in one eye, confusion)
- Headache that steadily worsens over days despite breaks
- New headache after age 50 accompanied by scalp tenderness or jaw pain with chewing
- Headache that appears only on one side and is accompanied by tearing, nasal congestion, or a drooping eyelid

These patterns are uncommon but should be checked by a clinician.

## Hidden Triggers That Make It Worse

- Wearing glasses with an outdated prescription so the eyes work harder
- Sitting with the top of the screen above eye level, forcing you to look up
- Air conditioning or a fan blowing directly on your face, drying the eyes further
- Skipping meals or going long stretches without water
- High stress or the social effort of “performing” on camera, which increases muscle tension
- Multiple calls in a row with almost no break between them

If you also notice dry or gritty eyes during ordinary screen time, the same mechanisms are usually at work. ## When to Worry

See a doctor if:

- The headache is sudden and severe
- It comes with vision changes, weakness, numbness, or trouble speaking
- It wakes you from sleep or is worse when you lie down
- You have a stiff neck, fever, or rash with the headache
- The pain is new, progressive, and not relieved by ordinary breaks and hydration
- You have a history of migraine and this feels different from your usual pattern

For the ordinary post-call ache that fades after you look into the distance, drink water, and move your neck, medical care is rarely needed.

## Myths vs Facts

**Myth:** Blue light from the screen is the main cause.  
**Fact:** Blue-light exposure can affect sleep later in the evening, but the headache during or right after a call is driven more by reduced blinking, sustained near focus, and neck posture than by blue light itself.

**Myth:** You need special computer glasses for every call.  
**Fact:** An up-to-date regular prescription, proper screen distance, and regular breaks usually matter more. Some people benefit from a mild plus-power or anti-reflective coating, but these are secondary.

**Myth:** If you get a headache after calls you must have migraines.  
**Fact:** Most of these headaches are tension-type or eye-strain related. They can coexist with migraine, but the post-call pattern alone does not diagnose migraine.

## How to Manage and Prevent It

Small changes during and between calls usually help:

- Follow the 20-20-20 idea: every 20 minutes look at something 20 feet away for 20 seconds. Even a quick glance out a window resets the focusing muscles.
- Consciously blink fully a few times each minute. A full blink spreads the tear film.
- Raise the laptop or use an external camera so the top of the screen is at or slightly below eye level. This keeps the neck more neutral. Neck position matters as much as the eyes; the same principles appear in [Is It Normal for Your Neck to Hurt After Reading in Bed?](https://research.betterhealthfacts.com/articles/is-it-normal-for-your-neck-to-hurt-after-reading-in-bed/).
- Soften the lighting. Avoid a bright window directly behind you and reduce overhead glare.
- Keep water nearby and sip through the call. A light snack beforehand can also steady blood sugar.
- Stand or walk for a minute between calls. Roll your shoulders and gently turn your head.
- If you wear contacts, consider glasses for long call days so the tear film is less stressed. Dry-eye strategies discussed in [Is It Normal for Your Eyes to Feel Dry After Screen Time?](https://research.betterhealthfacts.com/articles/is-it-normal-for-your-eyes-to-feel-dry-after-screen-time/) often overlap with headache prevention.
- After the call, look far away for a few minutes, do a few slow neck stretches, and step outside if possible.

Most people notice the headache lessens within days of these adjustments.

## When to See a Doctor

Book a routine visit if the headaches are frequent, interfere with work, or do not improve with the steps above. Seek urgent care for any sudden severe headache or one accompanied by neurological symptoms. An eye exam is worthwhile if you have not had one in the past year or two, especially if the ache is centered behind the eyes.

## FAQs

**Can a video call trigger a migraine?**  
Yes. For people who already have migraine, the combination of visual effort, neck tension, and stress can act as a trigger. The headache may then last longer and include light sensitivity or nausea.

**Will blue-light glasses stop the headache?**  
They may help some people with evening sleep or glare, but they do not address reduced blinking or neck posture, which are the larger drivers of the post-call ache.

**Is it better to turn the camera off?**  
Many people feel less pressure and hold better posture when the camera is off. If your workplace allows it, turning the camera off for part of a long meeting can reduce both visual and social load.

**How long should the headache last?**  
A typical eye-strain or tension headache from a call usually fades within 30–90 minutes after you stop looking at the screen, hydrate, and move. If it persists for many hours or days, other factors may be involved.

**Does caffeine help or hurt?**  
A modest amount can ease a withdrawal-related headache, but too much can increase muscle tension and later disrupt sleep. Staying consistent with your usual intake is usually better than large swings.

**Should I get my eyes checked?**  
Yes if it has been more than a year or two, or if the headache is mainly behind the eyes. An outdated prescription forces extra focusing effort.

## Conclusion

A headache after a long video call is normal for most people and usually reflects ordinary eye strain, reduced blinking, and sustained neck posture rather than a serious problem. Brief breaks to look into the distance, full blinks, a more neutral screen height, and steady hydration resolve it for the majority. If the pain is sudden and severe, comes with other neurological symptoms, or keeps returning despite these changes, a clinician can help rule out less common causes. For everyday calls, small adjustments to how you sit and look usually keep the ache from arriving in the first place.

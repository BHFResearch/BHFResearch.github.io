<p>Getting up from the floor or lowering into a deep squat feels tighter or less stable than it once did, even though you walk and sit every day. The hips still expect the occasional deep flexion that was a normal part of resting and working postures for most of human history.</p>

<h2>What This Expectation Feels Like</h2>
<p>People notice reduced ease when trying to sit on a low stool, pick something up from the floor without bending the back excessively, or hold a comfortable squat. The front of the hips or the deep hip joint can feel restricted, and the position may require more effort to maintain. Flat walking and ordinary chair sitting do not reproduce the same end-range demand.</p>

<h2>Why Your Hips Still Expect Occasional Deep Squats</h2>
<p>Ancestral resting and working postures frequently included deep hip flexion—squatting to rest, to cook, to socialize, or to work at ground level. The hip joint capsule, surrounding muscles, and cartilage adapted to regular visits to that range. Modern chair sitting keeps the hips near 90 degrees for long periods and rarely takes them into the deeper flexion of a full squat. Over time the tissues become less accustomed to the end range.</p>
<p>Anthropological and biomechanical observations, including work associated with Daniel Lieberman and others on human resting postures, note that deep squatting was common across many traditional societies. The hip complex still carries the structural expectation of occasional deep flexion because that loading pattern was ordinary for most of our evolutionary past.</p>

<h2>Common Modern Triggers</h2>
<p>Prolonged chair sitting, avoidance of floor sitting, and reliance on elevated toilets or chairs all reduce deep-hip exposure. When a deep position is finally required, the hips register the relative unfamiliarity as tightness or reduced control.</p>

<h2>When to Worry</h2>
<p>Mild restriction that improves with gradual practice is ordinary. Seek evaluation for sharp groin pain, catching or locking, significant asymmetry, or pain that limits walking. A history of hip injury or childhood hip conditions also warrants professional guidance before deep loading.</p>

<h2>How to Manage</h2>
<p>Gradual reintroduction works best:</p>
<ul>
<li>Practice supported partial squats while holding a counter or door frame, lowering only as far as comfortable.</li>
<li>Sit on a low sturdy stool or cushion for a few minutes several times a week.</li>
<li>Use a small step or box to practice controlled step-downs that encourage hip flexion.</li>
<li>Avoid forcing range; consistency with modest depth is more useful than occasional extreme efforts.</li>
</ul>

<h2>FAQs</h2>
<h3>Why do my hips feel fine walking but tight in a squat?</h3>
<p>Walking uses a limited arc of hip motion. Deep squatting asks for significantly more flexion than daily gait provides.</p>
<h3>Can I regain the range if I have been chair-sitting for years?</h3>
<p>Most people improve comfortable depth with gradual, regular practice, though individual joint structure sets some limits.</p>
<h3>Is deep squatting bad for the knees?</h3>
<p>When performed with control and within comfortable range, it is generally well tolerated. Pain is the signal to reduce depth.</p>

<h2>Conclusion</h2>
<p>Your hips still expect the occasional deep flexion that was once a routine posture. Modest, regular practice of supported squats or low sitting usually restores the ease the joints were shaped to provide.</p>

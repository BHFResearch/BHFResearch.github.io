<p>The ache sits in a place you cannot quite scratch. After a morning of laptop work, or a drive with the head pitched toward the road, a dull band gathers where the skull meets the neck. Looking up from the screen makes it pinch. Lying back on a firm pillow can feel like a bruise. Rubbing the hollows under the bone helps for a minute, then the weight of the head returns. Those hollows are home to the suboccipital muscles, a small deep set that still expects a level gaze and a stream of tiny corrections. Most desk days give them neither.</p>

<h2>What This Skull-Base Ache Feels Like</h2>

<p>People describe a pressure just under the occipital bone, often worse on one side, as if a thumb were pressing up into the skull. It can spread over the back of the head, toward the ear, or into a tight band across the forehead. Turning to check a blind spot feels rusty. A chin tuck may ease it, then a long email brings it back. Some notice the ache only when they finally look up, or when they rest the back of the head against a headrest. Sleep can be fine if the pillow keeps the neck neutral, and miserable if the chin juts or the head is cranked to one side.</p>

<p>A brief ache after a fixed head is common. Pain that shoots into the arm, true spinning, a sudden worst-ever headache, or weakness is not a simple muscle story.</p>

<h2>What the Suboccipitals Were Built to Do</h2>

<p>Four small pairs live in the suboccipital triangle and just beside it: rectus capitis posterior major, rectus capitis posterior minor, obliquus capitis superior, and obliquus capitis inferior. They run between the skull, the atlas, and the axis, the top two neck bones. Nikolai Bogduk’s maps of the cervical muscles treat them as fine controllers of the head on the upper neck, not as the engines of a big nod. Major and the superior oblique tip and side-bend the head. Inferior oblique turns the atlas on the axis. Minor is the shortest, from the atlas up to the skull.</p>

<p>They are packed with muscle spindles, the sensors that report length and speed. Work comparing spindle counts, including studies in the line of Peck, Buxton, and Nitz, found these small parallel muscles far richer in spindles than the big movers beside them. The job looks less like lifting the head and more like telling the brain exactly where the head sits while the eyes stay on a horizon. Walking over uneven ground, scanning a trail, and keeping the gaze level while the body rises and falls were daily work. Augustus White and Manohar Panjabi showed how much of head motion lives at those top joints. The suboccipitals are the local editors of that motion.</p>

<p>Gary Hack and colleagues, writing in Spine in 1995, described a connective-tissue bridge from rectus capitis posterior minor to the dura, the covering of the spinal cord. Later anatomical papers confirmed a myodural link in this region. It is fair to say the muscle is not isolated from the canal. It is not fair to claim every desk headache is a tug on the brain cover. The practical point is smaller: these muscles were built for brief adjustments and a level head, not for hours of a chin-forward hold.</p>

<h2>Why a Screen Day Leaves Them Short</h2>

<p>A level outdoor gaze puts the ears roughly over the shoulders and the eyes on a far point. A laptop or a phone drops the target. The head slides forward, the upper neck extends at the very top, and the lower neck flexes. In that pose the suboccipitals sit short at the skull base while the deeper neck flexors in front go quiet. Marie-Anne Mayoux-Benhamou and colleagues linked forward head posture with changes in the deep neck muscles. Gwendolen Jull and Deborah Falla showed that people with neck pain often lose fine control of the deep cervical flexors and overuse the superficial ones. The suboccipitals join that bargain: they hold a shortened length so the eyes can stay on a near screen.</p>

<p>Janet Travell and David Simons mapped referred pain from these muscles over the occiput, toward the eye, and sometimes into the forehead, the pattern many people call a tension headache after screen work. César Fernández-de-las-Peñas and colleagues later tied suboccipital trigger points to chronic tension-type headache. Richard Hallgren and colleagues reported smaller rectus capitis posterior major and minor in people with chronic neck pain, a hint that disuse and pain can shrink the very muscles meant to fine-tune the head. The mismatch is not that you looked at a screen once. It is that the gaze never returned to the distance, so the short hold became the default.</p>

<p>The larger neck story is the same bargain described in <a href="https://research.betterhealthfacts.com/articles/why-your-neck-still-expects-a-horizon/">why your neck still expects a horizon</a>. The suboccipitals are the deepest part of that bargain, the layer that aches when the horizon disappears. A glance behind, covered in <a href="https://research.betterhealthfacts.com/articles/why-your-splenius-still-expects-a-glance-behind/">why your splenius still expects a glance behind</a>, uses the strap muscles over them. If those turns are rare too, the whole back of the neck gets one job: holding a still head.</p>

<h2>Less Common but Serious Causes</h2>

<p>Most skull-base ache after desk work is muscle and joint load. A few patterns are not. A sudden severe headache, especially with neck stiffness, fever, confusion, or a new neurologic symptom, needs urgent care. Headache after a significant knock, a fall, or a whiplash that is getting worse rather than easing over days should be checked. Pain with jaw clenching can be the temporalis or the joint, not the suboccipitals. Vision change, a drooping lid, or headache that is strictly one-sided and new after age fifty is not a posture story. Arthritis at the upper neck, or irritation of the greater occipital nerve as it exits near these muscles, can mimic the ache and lasts past the workday.</p>

<h2>Hidden Triggers</h2>

<p>A low screen and a high chair stack the chin forward even when you feel upright. Bifocals and progressive lenses make some people tip the head back to find the reading zone, which shortens the same muscles from the other direction. A thick pillow that shoves the head forward all night continues the day pose. Clenching the jaw often recruits the skull base. Cold air on the neck after a still day can make the short muscles feel sharper, the way a quiet calf complains on the first hill. Caffeine withdrawal and skipped meals add a headache on top of the muscle ache, which is easy to blame only on posture. Long calls with the phone pinched between ear and shoulder load one side.</p>

<h2>When to Worry</h2>

<p>Get urgent help for a thunderclap headache, headache with fever and a stiff neck, new weakness, trouble speaking, double vision, fainting, or headache after a major injury. Book a clinician if the skull-base pain is waking you every night, spreading into the arm, coming with unexplained weight loss, or lasting more than a few weeks despite breaks and a better screen height. One-sided headache with scalp tenderness in a person over fifty deserves a prompt look. A mild ache that fades when you walk and look at the distance is the ordinary version.</p>

<h2>Myths vs Facts</h2>

<p><strong>Myth:</strong> Skull-base pain means a disc is out.<br>
<strong>Fact:</strong> Most of this ache is short deep muscle and upper-neck joint load from a forward head. Discs sit lower and usually declare themselves with arm pain or neurologic signs.</p>

<p><strong>Myth:</strong> Cracking the neck puts the muscles back.<br>
<strong>Fact:</strong> A pop is usually a joint cavitation. It does not lengthen a muscle that spends eight hours short. The pose has to change.</p>

<p><strong>Myth:</strong> A harder pillow is always better.<br>
<strong>Fact:</strong> The useful pillow fills the curve so the nose stays in line with the breastbone. Too high or too flat both park the suboccipitals at an odd length.</p>

<p><strong>Myth:</strong> You should stretch them hard several times a day.<br>
<strong>Fact:</strong> Aggressive chin-to-chest pulls irritate already unhappy muscle. Small nods, a higher screen, and time with the eyes on a far point do more.</p>

<h2>How to Give Them the Gaze They Expect</h2>

<p>Raise the screen so the top of the display is near eye level when you are tall in the chair, and bring the keyboard close so the chin does not chase the keys. Every half hour, look at something at least twenty feet away for twenty seconds and let the head sit back over the shoulders. A short walk outside is the original job: the eyes track a horizon, the head makes tiny corrections, and the muscles change length instead of holding one. Gentle range is enough. Nod yes and no slowly, five times each, without forcing the end. A chin tuck, as if you were making a soft double chin while looking forward, wakes the deep flexors Jull and Falla studied and gives the suboccipitals a longer rest. Stop if it creates sharp pain or dizziness.</p>

<p>At night, side sleeping with the nose in line with the chest, or back sleeping with a small support under the neck rather than a shove under the head, keeps the overnight length closer to neutral. Warmth on the skull base after a cold commute can ease the first pinch. If jaw clenching is part of the day, a few unclenched breaths help more than another neck stretch. The screen-day headache pattern in <a href="https://research.betterhealthfacts.com/articles/why-screen-days-give-you-a-headache/">why screen days give you a headache</a> often shares this setup: near gaze, still head, dry eyes, and a short skull base.</p>

<h2>When to See a Doctor</h2>

<p>See a clinician if the ache follows an injury, comes with arm numbness or weakness, includes true spinning when you roll in bed, or does not ease after two to three weeks of screen changes and walking breaks. A physical therapist can check whether the pain is suboccipital, upper-trap, or joint, and can teach a chin tuck that does not jam the neck. Mention new headache after fifty, scalp tenderness, jaw pain on chewing, or visual change so those are not folded into a posture explanation.</p>

<h2>FAQs</h2>

<h3>Why does the base of my skull hurt after computer work?</h3>
<p>A forward head shortens the suboccipital muscles while they hold the skull so your eyes stay on a near screen. The ache is usually that sustained short hold, plus stiff upper-neck joints, and it often eases once you look into the distance and let the head sit back.</p>

<h3>Are suboccipital muscles the same as the traps?</h3>
<p>No. The trapezius is the broad surface sheet from neck to shoulder. The suboccipitals are four small pairs under the skull, between the occiput, atlas, and axis. Trap ache sits more in the shoulder slope. Suboccipital ache sits in the hollow under the bone.</p>

<h3>Can these muscles cause a headache?</h3>
<p>They can refer a dull band over the back of the head and into the forehead, the pattern Travell mapped and later headache studies linked to suboccipital trigger points. That does not mean every headache is muscular. A new, severe, or neurologic headache still needs a proper look.</p>

<h3>Does a myodural bridge mean screen posture pulls on the brain?</h3>
<p>Hack’s 1995 description, and later anatomy papers, show connective tissue from rectus capitis posterior minor toward the dura. It explains why this region feels deeply connected. It is not proof that ordinary desk posture injures the spinal cord. Treating it as a reason for gentle motion, not alarm, fits the evidence.</p>

<h3>How often should I look up from the screen?</h3>
<p>A far gaze for about twenty seconds every half hour is a useful minimum, with a real walk once or twice in the work block. The muscles expect changing length and a level horizon, not a single stretch at the end of the day.</p>

<h3>Should I sleep without a pillow?</h3>
<p>Only if your neck stays neutral. Many people need a small fill so the head does not drop or jut. The test is morning: if the skull base is worse after sleep, the pillow height is part of the load.</p>

<h2>Conclusion</h2>

<p>The suboccipitals are not weak because you failed a workout. They are short because the gaze they were built for, a level look across open ground with constant tiny corrections, rarely shows up in a screen day. Raise the target, return the eyes to the distance, and let the head sit back over the ribs. The hollow under the skull usually remembers the job once the horizon comes back.</p>

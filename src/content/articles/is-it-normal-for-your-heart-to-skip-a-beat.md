You are sitting still and the next beat arrives early. There is a tiny pause. Then a heavier thump, as if the heart is catching up. Some people call it a skip. Some call it a flip, a flutter, or a hiccup in the chest. For a second the mind races toward the worst explanation. Then the rhythm settles and ordinary life continues.

That sensation is usually a **premature beat** — an extra electrical spark that fires a moment too soon. The “skip” you feel is often not a missing beat at all. It is the pause *after* the early beat, followed by a stronger contraction once a little extra blood has filled the chamber.

Occasional premature beats are among the most common findings in cardiology. They are frequently harmless in a structurally normal heart. They are also one of the most alarming sensations the body can produce, because the heart is the one organ we expect to be perfectly metronomic.

## What a Skipped Beat Actually Feels Like

People describe several related sensations:

- A single extra thump, then a brief pause
- A flip or roll in the chest or throat
- A cough-like catch that interrupts a breath
- Awareness of a stronger beat after a quiet gap
- A short run of flutters that lasts a few seconds

The feeling may be noticed more when you lie on the left side, sit quietly after coffee, or try to fall asleep. Movement and noise often hide the same beats that feel dramatic in a silent room.

A skipped beat is not the same as:

- A racing heart that stays fast for minutes
- Fainting or near-fainting
- Chest pressure with sweating and shortness of breath
- Hearing a pulse whoosh in time with each beat in one ear, which is a different pattern described in our guide to [hearing your heartbeat in your ear](https://research.betterhealthfacts.com/articles/is-it-normal-to-hear-your-heartbeat-in-your-ear)

Those other patterns deserve their own evaluation. Isolated extra beats usually do not.

## What Is Actually Happening

A normal heartbeat starts in the sinus node, a cluster of pacemaker cells in the right atrium. The impulse spreads through the atria, pauses in the AV node, then travels through the ventricles so they squeeze in order.

A **premature atrial contraction (PAC)** is an early spark from atrial tissue outside the sinus node. A **premature ventricular contraction (PVC)** is an early spark from the ventricle. Both reset the timing for a moment. After a PVC there is often a compensatory pause. The next normal beat then ejects a slightly larger volume of blood — which is why that beat can feel like a thud.

On a 12-lead ECG these events look brief and distinctive. On a 24-hour Holter monitor they are far more common than a snapshot ECG suggests.

Population studies put numbers on that gap. Reviews summarizing Holter data often cite premature ventricular beats in roughly 40 to 75 percent of people monitored for a day or two. A community study of more than 2,000 apparently healthy Swiss adults aged 25 to 41, published in *Heart* by Dr. David Conen and colleagues, found at least one PVC in 69 percent of 24-hour recordings. Most people had only a handful. The median count was two.

The Framingham Heart Study, using shorter ambulatory recordings, found that about one in three adults without clinically evident coronary disease had at least one ventricular premature beat per hour. The Atherosclerosis Risk in Communities (ARIC) study linked higher PVC counts on ECG with older age, male sex, hypertension, and lower serum magnesium or potassium.

The message from this body of work is consistent: finding an occasional extra beat on monitoring is ordinary. Frequency, symptoms, and the rest of the heart’s structure are what change the conversation.

## Why You Notice Some Beats and Not Others

The heart can fire extra beats that you never feel. Awareness rises when:

- You are still, especially lying on the left side
- Stroke volume of the post-pause beat is larger
- Anxiety turns attention inward
- Caffeine, alcohol, or poor sleep have increased extrasystoles
- The room is quiet enough that a thud feels like an event

Stress chemistry matters. Adrenaline shortens the time some heart cells need to recover, which makes an early spark more likely. That is one reason extra beats cluster after an argument, a late email, or a night of fragmented sleep — the same arousal system described in [the stress system your brain was built for](https://research.betterhealthfacts.com/articles/the-stress-system-your-brain-was-built-for).

Caffeine is a frequent suspect. Work from Dr. Gregory M. Marcus and colleagues at the University of California, San Francisco, including randomized n-of-1 monitoring in the CRAVE study, has shown that the relationship is more individual than folklore suggests. Some people clearly get more premature beats after coffee. Others do not. Alcohol, by contrast, has a more consistent link with atrial ectopy in several of Dr. Marcus’s analyses.

## Common Everyday Triggers

When extra beats cluster, the usual suspects are reversible:

- Coffee, energy drinks, or large doses of tea
- Alcohol, especially the evening after a few drinks
- Sleep loss and irregular sleep timing
- Dehydration
- A large meal, which can increase vagal tone and awareness of the heartbeat
- Fever or a recent viral illness
- High-intensity intervals in someone who is deconditioned
- Nicotine
- Certain cold remedies and decongestants that stimulate the sympathetic system

Electrolytes sit in the background. Low potassium or magnesium — after vomiting, heavy sweating, or some diuretics — can make ventricular ectopy more frequent. That is one reason clinicians check a basic metabolic panel when palpitations are new or clustered.

The gut-heart connection can confuse the picture. Coffee often sends people to the bathroom through a gastrocolic reflex, as explained in our article on [why coffee makes you need the bathroom](https://research.betterhealthfacts.com/articles/why-coffee-makes-you-need-the-bathroom). The same cup can also raise heart awareness. Two ordinary effects stacked in one morning can feel like one alarming syndrome.

## When Extra Beats Are Usually Reassuring

A typical benign pattern looks like this:

- Isolated flips rather than long runs
- No fainting, severe chest pain, or sudden breathlessness
- Beats that come and go with coffee, stress, or poor sleep
- A normal exam and, when checked, a normal ECG
- A heart that feels steady during exercise rather than more chaotic

In people without structural heart disease, occasional PACs and PVCs have long been treated as a nuisance more than a threat. Many right-ventricular-outflow-tract PVCs — a common “idiopathic” form mapped in electrophysiology labs led by physicians such as Dr. Francis E. Marchlinski — arise from otherwise healthy muscle and respond well if treatment is ever needed.

Even frequent PVCs can be manageable. The concern shifts when the *burden* is high (often discussed around 10 to 20 percent of all beats on a Holter) and the pumping function of the left ventricle begins to fall. That PVC-induced cardiomyopathy is uncommon among people who feel a skip once or twice a week. It is a reason frequent, daily palpitations still deserve a proper count rather than a shrug.

## Hidden Triggers People Miss

A few less obvious amplifiers:

- **Left-side lying.** Gravity and the heart’s contact with the chest wall make each extra beat more audible and more palpable.
- **Wearables.** A watch that flags “irregular rhythm” can turn a rare extra beat into a nightly worry loop, which then produces more adrenaline and more beats.
- **Thyroid overactivity.** Excess thyroid hormone increases ectopy. New palpitations plus heat intolerance or unexplained weight loss need a blood test.
- **Anemia.** A high-output circulation can make every beat more noticeable.
- **Reflux at night.** A burst of vagal activity after a late meal can produce a cluster of extra beats that feels cardiac even when the trigger is gastric.
- **New medications.** Some inhalers, ADHD stimulants, and decongestants raise the extrasystole rate.

## When to Worry

Seek prompt medical care — emergency care if the situation is severe — if skipped beats come with:

- Fainting or a true near-faint
- Crushing chest pressure, especially with sweating or pain into the arm or jaw
- Sudden severe shortness of breath
- A rapid irregular rhythm that does not settle within a few minutes
- New palpitations after fainting in a family with unexplained sudden death
- Dizziness that prevents standing safely

Schedule a non-urgent visit if:

- Extra beats happen daily and last weeks
- You can count long strings rather than single thumps
- Exercise now produces more skips, not fewer
- There is known heart disease, a murmur, or reduced pumping function
- You are pregnant and the pattern is new and intense

A clinician may order an ECG, blood tests, a Holter or event monitor, and sometimes an echocardiogram. Those tools separate a noisy but healthy heart from a heart that needs treatment.

## Myths vs Facts

**Myth:** A skipped beat means the heart is about to stop.  
**Fact:** The pause is usually the recovery interval after an early beat. The next beat is typically stronger, not weaker.

**Myth:** Any extra beat on a watch is atrial fibrillation.  
**Fact:** Isolated PACs and PVCs are far more common. Atrial fibrillation is a sustained irregular rhythm. Consumer devices can confuse the two; an ECG strip is the referee.

**Myth:** Coffee always causes dangerous palpitations.  
**Fact:** Sensitivity is personal. Many people tolerate ordinary coffee. A two-week trial off caffeine is a fair experiment, not a lifelong ban for everyone.

**Myth:** If you can feel it, it must be serious.  
**Fact:** Awareness tracks attention, body position, and stroke volume as much as danger. Plenty of serious rhythms are silent. Plenty of harmless ones feel dramatic.

**Myth:** Exercise must be stopped the moment a skip appears.  
**Fact:** In a cleared, structurally normal heart, regular activity is often associated with *fewer* PVCs. The Swiss healthy-adult analysis found higher physical activity linked with a lower PVC count.

## How to Settle Extra Beats

Most people do not need a procedure. Practical steps help:

1. **Log the pattern for two weeks.** Note time of day, caffeine, alcohol, sleep, and whether you were lying on your left side. Patterns are more useful than panic.
2. **Trim the obvious stimulants.** Test a lower-caffeine week. Keep alcohol modest, especially in the evening.
3. **Protect sleep.** Extra beats love 1 a.m. worry. A consistent wind-down beats another hour of scrolling.
4. **Rehydrate and eat regular meals.** Large swings in fluid and electrolytes make the heart more irritable.
5. **Move daily.** A walk after meals and some unhurried aerobic time often quiet ectopy more than complete rest.
6. **Change position.** If left-side lying is the amplifier, try the right side or a slightly more upright sleep posture.
7. **Slow the breath when a cluster starts.** A longer exhale recruits vagal tone and gives the next minute a calmer backdrop. It does not “cure” a PVC, but it stops the adrenaline spiral that produces the next ten.

If symptoms persist, a clinician can discuss beta blockers for awareness, or — when the burden is high and the heart muscle is affected — referral to an electrophysiologist for mapping and ablation of a focal source.

## When to See a Doctor

See a doctor if extra beats are new and frequent, if they change with exertion in a worrying way, or if you have risk factors such as high blood pressure, diabetes, known valve disease, or a strong family history of early heart disease. Bring a wearable strip if you have one, but do not treat the watch as a diagnosis.

A first visit is often straightforward: history, exam, ECG, and a plan for monitoring. Most people leave with reassurance and a short list of triggers to test. A smaller group leaves with a useful next test. Both outcomes are better than another month of silent catastrophizing at 2 a.m.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is a skipped heartbeat the same as a heart attack?**  
No. A heart attack is a blood-flow emergency, usually with pressure, sweating, or breathlessness. An isolated flip with an otherwise steady rhythm is a timing event, not a blocked artery. New chest pressure still needs urgent care — do not use this article to talk yourself out of that.

**Why does it happen more at night?**  
Quiet rooms, left-side lying, and a slight rise in vagal tone all make extra beats easier to feel. Sleep loss and evening alcohol add fuel.

**Can anxiety cause real extra beats, or only the feeling of them?**  
Both. Anxiety increases awareness *and* can increase true ectopy through adrenaline. The ECG does not lie: many anxious people have documented PACs or PVCs, not imagined ones.

**Do I need to stop exercising?**  
Not automatically. If skips come with fainting, severe dizziness, or chest pain, stop and get evaluated. If they are isolated and you have been cleared, regular moderate activity is often helpful.

**Will this turn into atrial fibrillation?**  
Frequent PACs have been associated, in research including work involving Dr. Marcus and others, with a higher later risk of atrial fibrillation in some groups. That is a reason to treat sleep, alcohol, and blood pressure well. It is not a prediction that one skip tonight becomes AF tomorrow.

**Can children have skipped beats?**  
Yes. Isolated PVCs in otherwise healthy children are often watched and frequently improve. Pediatric cardiology decides when an echo or longer monitor is needed.

## Conclusion

A skipped beat is usually an extra beat wearing a dramatic costume. The pause and the following thump are mechanics, not a verdict on your future. Occasional PACs and PVCs show up in a large share of healthy adults once anyone bothers to look for a full day.

Notice the pattern. Change the obvious inputs. Get checked when the company is fainting, pain, true breathlessness, or a daily storm that will not settle. The goal is not a heart that never makes an extra spark. The goal is a heart you understand well enough that one flip no longer feels like the end of the story.

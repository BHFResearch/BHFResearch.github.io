You start dinner. The first slice through a raw onion sends a faint sharp scent across the board. A few seconds later the eyes sting, the lids squeeze, and tears well up as if you had just heard bad news. You are not sad. You are just cooking.

Yes — it is normal. Onion tears are reflex tears, the same wash-the-surface system that fires when wind, dust, or [spicy-food vapor](https://research.betterhealthfacts.com/articles/is-it-normal-to-get-a-runny-nose-from-spicy-food/) hits sensitive nerves. The onion is not attacking your feelings. It is releasing a volatile defense chemical that your cornea is extremely good at noticing.

## What Onion Tears Feel Like

The pattern is chemical and fast:

- A sharp, almost peppery sting that starts a few seconds after the first cut
- Watering that builds as you keep chopping, then eases when you step back
- Both eyes involved, even if you lean over one side of the board
- A runny nose that often arrives with the tears
- Blurry vision until you blink the extra fluid away
- No heaviness in the chest, no story, no leftover mood — just wet lashes and a cutting board

The sting can feel stronger with a freshly harvested pungent onion than with a milder stored one. Sweet varieties and some “tearless” cultivars still smell like onion; they simply make less of the gas that reaches your eyes.

## Why an Onion Makes You Cry

An intact onion is a locked chemistry set. Flavor precursors called S-alk(en)yl cysteine sulfoxides sit in one part of the cell. An enzyme called alliinase sits in another. As long as the cells stay whole, those two stay apart.

The knife changes that.

**Step one — the cut mixes the kit.** Alliinase meets a precursor abundant in onion, often called PRENCSO or trans-S-1-propenyl-L-cysteine sulfoxide. The first product is a reactive intermediate: 1-propenyl sulfenic acid.

**Step two — a second enzyme makes the tear gas.** For decades, chemists assumed that sulfenic acid rearranged on its own into the irritant. In 2002, Shinsuke Imai and colleagues at House Foods Corporation, working with Toshiyuki Nagata at the University of Tokyo, showed in *Nature* that a previously unknown enzyme does the job. They named it lachrymatory-factor synthase, or LFS. LFS converts the sulfenic acid into syn-propanethial S-oxide — also written as (Z)-propanethial S-oxide — the classic onion lachrymatory factor.

That finding mattered. Garlic has alliinase too, and garlic is pungent, but garlic does not run the same LFS step. That is one reason you can mince garlic without the same flood of tears.

**Step three — the gas meets the cornea.** Syn-propanethial S-oxide is volatile. It leaves the board, reaches the film of moisture on the eye, and stimulates free nerve endings in the cornea. Those nerves are among the densest pain sensors in the body. Work mapped by Juana Gallar, Carlos Belmonte, and M. Carmen Acosta has shown how corneal polymodal fibers drive a brisk lacrimal reflex. The brain reads the signal as “rinse this off,” not as grief. The main lacrimal glands under the outer brow dump extra fluid. Some of it overflows the lid. Some of it drains into the nose, which is why onion chopping so often comes with a sniffle.

A popular kitchen myth says the gas turns into sulfuric acid on the eye. The real sequence is simpler and well measured: a volatile sulfur oxide stimulates corneal nerves, and reflex tears follow. You do not need a drop of industrial acid to explain dinner.

Eric Block and other organosulfur chemists have spent decades tracing how *Allium* plants use these same pathways as chemical defense. The tear gas is not a kitchen accident. It is part of the plant’s answer to being bitten, crushed, or sliced.

## What Onion Tears Are Not

They are not emotional crying. Psychologist Ad Vingerhoets and colleagues who study human tears distinguish basal lubrication, reflex washing, and emotional crying. Onion vapor uses the reflex route. Limbic “I am sad” circuits are not required.

They are not an onion allergy in the usual sense. A true food allergy involves the immune system and can include hives, swelling, wheeze, or gut symptoms after eating. Stinging while you chop, then feeling fine once the vapor clears, is irritation, not IgE.

They are not the same as [tears that appear when you yawn](https://research.betterhealthfacts.com/articles/is-it-normal-to-get-tears-when-you-yawn/). Yawn tears are mostly squeeze and drainage. Onion tears are a chemical reflex from the cornea.

They are not proof that your eyes are damaged. Brief sting and watering after a pungent cut is the system doing its job. Persistent pain, light sensitivity, or a scratchy feeling that lasts hours after you leave the kitchen is a different story and deserves a look.

## Why Some Onions Are Worse Than Others

Pungency and tear power are related but not identical.

Fresh, high-sulfur onions grown in rich soil often make more precursor and more LFS product. Long-stored “sweet” onions can be milder because some of the precursor chemistry has already shifted. The root end and the core of a freshly cut bulb often seem stronger than the first dry outer layers, simply because more intact cells are opening at once.

Researchers later used RNA interference to quiet the LFS gene. Colin Eady and collaborators in New Zealand showed that silencing LFS slashed the tear gas and pushed more of the sulfenic acid toward thiosulfinates — the family of compounds that carry a lot of fresh-onion flavor. That work helped explain why a “tearless” onion is possible in principle, and why flavor and tears are cousins rather than twins. Commercial milder onions use breeding and storage more often than gene silencing, but the biochemistry is the same map.

Shallots and some leeks can sting. Garlic usually does not, because it lacks the same LFS-dominated route to propanethial S-oxide.

## Everyday Tricks That Actually Change the Dose

You cannot repeal plant chemistry, but you can change how much gas reaches the eye.

**Chill the onion.** Cold slows the enzymes. A brief rest in the refrigerator before chopping lowers the burst of vapor without ruining texture if you do not freeze the bulb.

**Use a sharp knife.** A dull blade crushes a wider band of cells. A sharp blade opens fewer cells per slice and throws less aerosol into the air.

**Ventilate.** A window, a fan that pulls air across the board and away from your face, or a range hood on low does more than a folk remedy. The irritant is a gas. Moving air dilutes it.

**Cut under a gentle stream or next to running water.** Water can capture some of the vapor before it rises. You do not need to stand in a cloud of steam.

**Do not lean over the board.** Distance is a simple dose reducer. So is looking slightly away between cuts.

**Goggles work; ordinary glasses help only a little.** Tight-fitting kitchen or swim goggles block the path to the cornea. Everyday spectacles leave gaps at the sides and top.

**Rinse the cut surfaces.** A quick rinse of the halves after the first cut washes some of the freshly generated film off the face of the onion.

Tricks that are weaker than they sound: a slice of bread in the mouth, a candle on the counter, chewing gum, or lighting a match. If they help, it is usually because you slowed down, stepped back, or ventilated — not because the bread absorbed the molecule.

Contact-lens wearers sometimes notice more sting because the lens can hold a thin film of irritant against the surface. Taking a step back and blinking more often is kinder than rubbing.

## Hidden Triggers and Look-Alikes

A few kitchen patterns get blamed on “sensitive eyes” when the real issue is dose or a second irritant:

- Chopping a large pile at once, so the vapor keeps rising
- A small closed kitchen with no airflow
- Cutting onion and chili in the same session — capsaicin can add its own [gustatory and airborne sting](https://research.betterhealthfacts.com/articles/is-it-normal-to-get-a-runny-nose-from-spicy-food/)
- Wind from an open window blowing the plume into your face instead of away
- Already irritated eyes from dry indoor air, wind, or a cold — the same surface that [waters in a stiff breeze](https://research.betterhealthfacts.com/articles/is-it-normal-for-your-eyes-to-water-in-the-wind/) will also protest onion vapor sooner
- Rubbing the eyes with onion-scented fingers, which puts residue on the lids

If watering starts before you cut anything, look at steam, pepper, cleaning spray, or an allergy day rather than the onion.

## When to Worry

See an eye-care professional or urgent clinic if:

- Pain is severe, or the eye stays red and light-sensitive long after you leave the kitchen
- Vision stays blurred, or you see new flashes, a curtain, or a sudden shower of [floaters](https://research.betterhealthfacts.com/articles/is-it-normal-to-see-floaters-in-your-vision/)
- The lids swell dramatically, or you develop hives, wheeze, or throat tightness after handling or eating onion — that pattern is not ordinary chopping irritation
- One eye only is involved and feels scratched, as if a piece of onion or a contact lens edge is still there
- Watering and sting happen with many foods, soaps, or winds, and daily life is limited

Ordinary onion tears fade within minutes of fresh air. Anything that lingers is asking a different question.

## Myths vs Facts

**Myth:** Onions make sulfuric acid in your eyes.  
**Fact:** They release syn-propanethial S-oxide. Corneal nerves trigger reflex tears. You do not need a sulfuric-acid story to explain the sting.

**Myth:** Only “weak” people cry over onions.  
**Fact:** Corneal nerve density is high in everyone. Dose, airflow, onion type, and whether you lean over the board explain most of the difference.

**Myth:** If garlic does not make you cry, onion sting must be an allergy.  
**Fact:** Garlic and onion share alliinase but not the same LFS-dominated tear-gas step. Different chemistry, same kitchen.

**Myth:** A tearless onion has no flavor.  
**Fact:** Imai’s group and later silencing studies showed that LFS and flavor thiosulfinates sit on related branches. You can lower the tear gas without erasing every sulfur note, though the profile does change.

**Myth:** Holding a piece of bread in your mouth absorbs the fumes.  
**Fact:** The gas is in the air around your eyes, not mainly in your mouth.

## How to Manage Onion Tears

Treat it as ventilation plus dose, not as a personality test.

- Chill the bulb, use a sharp knife, and keep your face out of the plume
- Turn on a fan or open a window so air moves across the board and away
- Wear close-fitting goggles if you chop large amounts or have easily irritated eyes
- Rinse the knife and the cut faces once or twice as you work
- Step away for thirty seconds if the sting builds; reflex tears reset quickly in fresh air
- Wash hands before touching your face
- If you wear contacts, consider glasses for heavy onion prep

None of this requires special products. It is the same logic as standing upwind of campfire smoke.

## When to See a Doctor

Book a routine eye exam if onion vapor is only the loudest version of daily watering, grit, or light sensitivity. Dry-eye surface problems and allergies make every irritant feel bigger.

Seek same-day care for one-sided severe pain, a feeling that something is stuck, sudden vision change, or swelling that looks like an allergic reaction rather than a few extra blinks.

If eating onion — not just chopping it — causes mouth tingling, hives, or breathing difficulty, that belongs with an allergy-aware clinician, not with cutting-board tips.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is it normal to cry every time I cut an onion?**  
Yes. Many people do. A pungent fresh onion in a still kitchen is a high dose of a real corneal stimulant. Changing airflow and knife habits often cuts the tears more than “toughening up.”

**Why does my nose run when I chop onions?**  
Extra tears drain through the nasolacrimal ducts into the nose. The same vapor can also irritate the nasal lining a little. The sniffle is plumbing plus a mild reflex, not a winter cold.

**Do sweet onions never make you cry?**  
They usually make you cry less, not never. Lower precursor levels and storage change the burst. A very large pile of “sweet” onion in a closed room can still sting.

**Is it better to cut onions under water?**  
A little water on the board or a rinse of the halves can help. Fully submerging the work is messy and can dull flavor. Chill plus ventilation is usually enough.

**Can onion vapor damage my eyes?**  
Brief reflex tearing is protective, not injurious. Rubbing hard with onion on your fingers, or leaving an irritant trapped under a contact lens, is the part to avoid. Persistent pain is not normal leftover sting.

**Why can I mince garlic but not onion?**  
Garlic uses alliinase for flavor sulfur compounds but does not rely on onion’s lachrymatory-factor synthase to flood the air with propanethial S-oxide. Related plants, different last step.

## Conclusion

Crying over an onion is not a mood and not a flaw. It is a plant defense molecule meeting one of the most sensitive surfaces on the body. Shinsuke Imai’s team showed that a dedicated enzyme, not a kitchen myth, builds that molecule. Your lacrimal glands then do exactly what they evolved to do: wash the surface and invite you to step back.

Chill the bulb, sharpen the knife, move the air, and keep onion fingers off the eyelids. Dinner can stay pungent. Your eyes do not have to file a complaint every time.

You open wide for a yawn and the corner of your mouth stings. A small split has appeared where the lip meets the cheek, sometimes on both sides, sometimes only the side you sleep on. Tomato sauce finds it. A smile finds it. By evening you have licked it again, and the lick made it worse.

That pattern is common. Clinicians call the sore spot angular cheilitis, and older writing calls it perlèche. It is not the same thing as lips that chap across the middle in dry indoor air. The corner is a fold. Folds hold moisture. Moisture softens skin that was never meant to stay wet, and a crack follows.

## What Corner Cracks Feel Like

The spot is small and oddly specific. Redness sits right at the commissure, the angle where the upper and lower lip meet. A shallow fissure may cross that angle. The skin around it looks pale, soggy, or lightly crusted, as if a drop of saliva has been sitting there. Opening the mouth, biting an apple, or wiping with a napkin all tug the split.

Some people notice it after a run of mouth breathing at night, a cold that stuffed the nose, a new habit of licking, or a week of very dry heated air. Others notice it when dental work, a night guard, or a missing back tooth changes how the lips sit. It can be one-sided. It is often both sides. It usually does not start as a cluster of blisters on the pink of the lip.

Pain is out of proportion to the size. A fissure a few millimeters long can make talking uncomfortable. That is anatomy, not drama. The corner moves every time the jaw opens.

## Why the Corner Splits

The lip surface and the nearby cheek skin are thin. At the commissure they fold against each other, especially if the bite has closed down a little with age, dentures, or worn teeth. Saliva pools in that fold. At night, if you breathe through your mouth or drool toward one cheek, the pool lasts for hours.

Wet skin swells, loses its barrier, and fissures when it is stretched. Once the barrier is open, microbes that already live in the mouth move in. Oral pathologists have long found that angular cheilitis is often a mixed local problem, not a single germ. Sven Ohman and colleagues, sampling commissure lesions, recovered Candida yeasts in a large share of cases, often with Staphylococcus aureus in the same cracks. Either organism can keep a fissure from sealing. Together they do it more reliably.

Roy Rogers, reviewing the condition as a sign rather than a diagnosis by itself, stressed the same point. The corner is telling you that moisture, a deep fold, or a colonizing microbe has set up shop. It is not automatically telling you that a vitamin is missing.

Dry indoor air still matters, but in a roundabout way. Chapped lips invite licking. Licking wets the corners. The center of the lip may look dry while the angles look soggy. That combination is why a [chap from heated rooms](https://research.betterhealthfacts.com/articles/is-it-normal-for-your-lips-to-chap-in-dry-indoor-air/) and a corner crack often arrive in the same winter, for different reasons.

## Common Everyday Triggers

Lip licking is the loop most people can feel. The crack stings, the tongue wets it, the sting eases for a minute, and the skin stays macerated. Flavored balms and mint can make the urge stronger.

Mouth breathing does the overnight version. A blocked nose, a habit of sleeping with the lips parted, or a very dry room leaves saliva at the angles by morning. People who [wake with a dry mouth](https://research.betterhealthfacts.com/articles/is-it-normal-to-wake-up-with-a-dry-mouth/) or who [drool on the pillow](https://research.betterhealthfacts.com/articles/is-it-normal-to-drool-when-you-sleep/) are describing the same airway story from two sides. One side of the mouth dries. The downhill corner stays wet.

Orthodontic brackets, a new retainer, and wide smiles during a speech-heavy week all tug the commissure. So does a mask that sits damp against the angles for hours, a pattern many people noticed when face coverings were constant. Thumb-sucking or pacifier use does the childhood version, with saliva held in the fold.

A deep crease makes recurrence more likely. Dentures that have lost height, worn molars, or missing back teeth let the bite overclose. The lips fold inward. MacFarlane and other oral-medicine writers described this commissure fold as a local setup for perlèche in denture wearers. The microbe is secondary. The crease is the room it rents.

## Less Common but Real Causes

Iron deficiency, low vitamin B12, folate deficiency, and experimental riboflavin deficiency can all show up at the mouth corners. William Sebrell and Roy Butler, in early nutrition studies, produced angular lesions when riboflavin was removed from the diet. Those links are real. They are also not the usual explanation in someone who eats regularly and has a licking habit or a wet pillow.

Recurrent cracks plus tiredness, a sore tongue, pale inner eyelids, or a diet very low in animal foods and fortified grains are a better reason to ask about iron and B12 than a single winter fissure. Zinc deficiency is discussed in the same oral-sign lists, and it is uncommon outside clear dietary restriction or malabsorption.

Diabetes and other states that favor yeast can turn a small split into a stubborn one. So can inhaled steroids that deposit in the mouth, broad antibiotics, and isotretinoin, which dries the lip surface and leaves the corners fragile. A clinician who already knows you are on one of those treatments will often recognize the pattern.

A corner ulcer that is hard, grows, or refuses to heal is a different problem. Sun-damaged lower lips and persistent one-sided sores belong in a clinic, not in a balm experiment. Herpes can occasionally sit near a commissure, but a classic cold sore is a blister cluster on the lip or nearby skin, often with tingling first, not a chronic soggy split.

## When to Worry

Most corner cracks are a local skin problem. A few patterns deserve a look rather than another week of licking.

See a clinician promptly if a crack is one-sided and thickening, if the area bleeds without being stretched, or if it has not improved after about two weeks of keeping it dry and protected. Spreading redness onto the cheek, honey-colored crust beyond the fold, fever, or trouble opening the mouth are reasons not to wait.

Ask sooner if cracks keep returning and you also have fatigue, mouth burning, a smooth sore tongue, or known anemia. Children with deep commissure splits, and anyone with white patches inside the cheeks that scrape off, should be seen. Those white patches can be thrush, and the corner may be the same yeast.

Sudden swelling of the lip itself, hives, or trouble breathing is not angular cheilitis. That is an allergy timeline.

## Myths vs Facts

Myth: Cracked corners always mean a B-vitamin deficiency. Fact: Moisture, licking, and Candida or skin bacteria explain most ordinary cases. Deficiency is a cause, not the default.

Myth: If it is yeast, you must have caught an infection from someone. Fact: Candida already lives in many healthy mouths. A wet fold simply lets it overgrow.

Myth: Antibacterial ointment alone is always the fix. Fact: If yeast is the main colonizer, an antibiotic ointment can miss it. If bacteria dominate, an antifungal alone can miss them. A clinician can match the ointment to the look of the lesion, and sometimes to a swab.

Myth: The more you wet the crack, the faster it seals. Fact: Saliva is the setup. Drying the fold and sealing it from the next lick is the usual first step.

Myth: It is a cold sore, so it will run a fixed course. Fact: Cold sores crust and finish. A commissure fissure can linger for weeks if the fold stays wet.

## How to Manage Corner Cracks

Pat the angles dry after eating and after brushing. Do not rub. A plain occlusive ointment, such as white petrolatum, applied in a thin film after drying, keeps the next wave of saliva off the split. Use it at bedtime especially. Flavored and menthol balms feel nicer and often sting more.

Stop the lick, including the unconscious one while you read. If the center of the lip is dry, treat that too, so the tongue has less reason to tour the mouth. Sipping water is fine. Coating the crack with saliva is not.

Nasal breathing at night reduces the drool-and-dry combination. A brief saline rinse, treating a stuffy nose, and a room that is not desert-dry all help. If one cheek is always wet on the pillow, that is the side to protect before sleep.

Skip acidic and salty foods against the split for a few days. Orange juice, tomato, and vinegar are informative: they light the fissure up because nerve endings are exposed, not because citrus caused it.

If the crack is bright red, soggy, and still there after a week of dryness and petrolatum, a clinician may suggest a short course aimed at yeast, bacteria, or both. Do not stack leftover steroid creams on the corner. Steroid alone, on a yeast-heavy fold, can widen the red area.

Denture wearers should have the fit and the bite height checked if cracks return every winter. A crease deep enough to hold saliva will outlast any tube of ointment. Aligners and retainers should be cleaned, and the corners dried after they come out.

People on isotretinoin usually need a plainer, thicker barrier and a conversation with the prescriber if fissures are severe. People using inhaled steroids can rinse and spit after each dose so less medicine sits at the angles.

## When to See a Doctor

Go if the split is worsening, one-sided and persistent, or paired with white patches inside the mouth, fever, or facial redness that is spreading. Go if cracks recur for months without a clear licking or drooling habit. Blood tests for iron, B12, and folate are reasonable then, not on the first dry Monday of heating season.

A dentist is the right next stop when dentures, missing molars, or a collapsed bite seem to fold the lip inward. An oral-medicine clinician can sort yeast, bacteria, and the rare sore that does not belong in the commissure at all.

## FAQs

### Is it normal for only one corner to crack?

Yes. People often drool toward one cheek, lick one side, or hold a phone or a pen against one commissure. One-sided pain that crusts and heals is common. One-sided thickening that does not heal should be examined.

### Are cracked corners contagious?

The yeast and skin bacteria involved are usually your own. Ordinary contact does not pass a commissure crack the way a fresh cold sore can pass herpes. Still, do not share lip balm while the skin is open.

### Can kids get this from a pacifier?

Yes. A pacifier or a thumb holds saliva in the fold. Dry the corners, shorten pacifier time if you can, and use a plain barrier at night. Deep or recurrent splits in a child deserve a clinician, partly to check for thrush and partly because licking habits linger.

### Does spicy food cause it?

Spicy and acidic food irritate a split that is already open. They are a poor match for the healing week. They are rarely the reason the fold broke in the first place.

### How long should a corner crack take to close?

Once saliva is kept off it, a shallow fissure often calms in several days and seals within one to two weeks. A crack that looks the same after two weeks of dryness and a plain ointment is no longer a wait-and-see problem.

## Conclusion

A sting at the corner of the mouth is usually a small fold that stayed wet. Saliva, a night of mouth breathing, and a lick each time it burns are enough to keep skin from sealing. Yeast and ordinary skin bacteria move into that opening often enough that Ohman’s swabs and Rogers’s clinical reviews both treat them as part of the local story, not as proof of a rare infection.

Vitamin deficiency belongs on the list, not at the top of it. Dry the angle, seal it with a plain ointment, ease the licking, and give the fold a week without tomato juice. If it will not close, or if it keeps returning with tiredness or a sore tongue, that is the moment for a clinician, not for a stronger lip balm. The corner is small. It is also honest about what has been sitting in it.
